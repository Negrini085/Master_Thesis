\section{Glaciated areas}

The degree day model developed within the framework of this thesis produces daily snow water equivalent maps according to Equation \ref{eq:swe_swe}. Since 
the SWE variable is set to zero only at the beginning of the simulation and is not reset thereafter, any snow remaining at the end of the season is carried 
over to the following year. Consequently, in high elevation alpine catchments where temperature do not allow complete snowmelt, pixels can keep accumulating 
snow for multiple years. In particular, grid points located above \SI{3500}{\meter} a.s.l. appear to experience continuous snow cover throughout the 
whole \num{72} years period between \num{1951} and \num{2023}, as illustrated in panel (a) of Figure \ref{fig:csc_vs_ele}. Moreover, a significant share 
of pixels above \SI{3000}{\meter} a.s.l. appear to experience all year round snow coverage at least once.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{results/csc_vs_ele.png}
    \caption{(a) Number of years with snow cover all year round as a function of elevation. Each dot is a grid point of the simulated domain. (b) Distribution 
    of cells with at least one year of continuous snow cover as a function of elevation.}
    \label{fig:csc_vs_ele}
\end{figure}

Although this phenomenon is completely consistent with snow dynamics in the Alps, as it represents one of the main processes governing glacier ice 
formation, it requires appropriate handling to avoid incorrect estimates of seasonal snow cover dynamics and trends over the study area. Indeed, 
since grid points are treated independently, snow can accumulate indefinitely at higher elevations, leading to unrealistically deep snowpacks that persist 
throughout the simulation. A possible solution to this issue would be to develop a glacier module that simulates the transformation of snow into firn and 
ice, as well as glacier flow, thereby allowing mass to be transferred downward to milder thermal conditions.  However, this is beyond the scope of this 
thesis. The adopted solution was instead to exclude from SWE calculations those pixels that remained snow covered for at least half of the investigated 
period, i.e., \num{36} out of {72} years. These grid points are located on the most important massifs of the Italian Alps, as illustrated in panel (a) of 
Figure \ref{fig:covered_area_maps}, and roughly resemble the average glacier extent over the 1951--2023 period. The masked area accounts for approximately 
\SI{423}{\kilo\meter\squared}, representing \num{0.27}\% of the study area.

\section{SWE predictions}

After masking the daily SWE maps to exclude glacier-covered areas, the total volume of water stored in the 
snowpack was estimated on a daily basis. The resulting time series, shown in Figure \ref{fig:masked_swe_evolution}, exhibits 
substantial interannual variability, reflecting the sensitivity of snow accumulation and duration to different meteorological 
patterns. Notably, throughout the 1975--1985 period, a significant amount of SWE persisted untill the end of summer, indicating 
relatively favorable conditions for the cryosphere. This behavior is also consistent with the glacier advance observed in the 
Italian Alps during the same period, which was driven by reduced summer ablation and enhanced winter accumulation 
\parencite{only_advance_italian_alps}.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{results/masked_swe_evolution.png}
    \caption{Daily evolution of the total water volume stored as snow in the IH-GAR from 1 October 1951 to 30 September 2023.}
    \label{fig:masked_swe_evolution}
\end{figure}
Even a visual inspection of the time series suggests that markedly below average conditions become more frequent toward the end of 
the 1951--2023 period: notably, all four snow seasons peaking below $\SI{4}{\giga\meter\cubed}$ occurred since 2000. To investigate 
this behavior more systematically, both the annual maximum (peak) SWE and the 1 April SWE values were extracted for each 
hydrological year, providing the basis for both a climatological characterization and a temporal trend assessment. Over the 
1991--2020 reference period, the mean annual peak SWE in the IH-GAR is $7.4 \pm 3.1\,\SI{}{\giga\meter\cubed}$, 
typically occuring on -calcolare data picco-. As this date precedes 1 April, the 1 April climatological value is lower than the 
maximum one as a consequence of the onset of snowmelt, amounting to . As regards the temporal evolution, the presence of monotonic 
trends in both peak SWE and 1 April SWE was assessed over the 1951--2023 period by means of the Mann--Kendall test, with trend 
magnitude evaluated thanks to Sen--Theil estimator. Although a substantial spread is present as a consequence of interannual 
variability, both metrics exhibit statistically significant negative trends ($p\,=\,0.003$ and $p\,=\,0.006$), as shown in Figure 
\ref{fig:peak_fap_trends}. In particular, peak and 1 April SWE decreased respectively by \SI{0.567}{\giga\meter\cubed}/decade 
and \SI{0.506}{\giga\meter\cubed}/decade over the investigated period.

    \label{fig:peak_fap_trends}
\end{figure}
Since the total volume of water stored within the snowpack is an aggregate quantity, its decline do not reveal which 
regions have experienced the largest losses. As a first step towards a deeper understanding of these trends, the study area was 
partitioned into six elevation bands: the first five were \SI{500}{\meter} wide, while the sixth one comprised every pixel located 
above $\SI{2500}{\meter}$. Elevation-wise SWE series were subsequently computed to extract peak SWE and 1 April SWE for each 
band and each hydrological year on record, allowing for the application of the Mann--Kendall test and the Sen--Theil estimator 
to assess the role of elevation in the observed decline of snow cover. The results of these analyses are reported in Table 
\ref{tab:peak_swe_model_trends} and \ref{tab:first_april_swe_model_trends}, with trends expressed as percentage changes per decade 
relative to the 1952--1981 climatological mean of each band. Although for both metrics the reduction is not confined to a specific 
altitude range, its relative magnitude decreases with elevation. Such a pattern is consistent with the higher sensitivity of snow 
cover to rising temperatures where these are closer to the freezing point, since even a modest warming may increase the fraction of 
precipitation falling as rain, thus reducing accumulation, and may favour earlier snowmelt, as mild conditions occur earlier in 
the season.

The presence of this positive trends in February and March is thought to be related to an increase in snow accumulation, as global 
warming allows air to hold more water vapour, which may result in locally-enhanced precipitations during winter. However, this 
positive trends gradually weaken towards the beginning of April, as increased temperatures also cause an earlier onset of snowmelt 
that progressively counterbalance the positive effects of enhanced snowfall. Nevertheless, once the shift to negative trend occurs, 
its magnitude remains considerably smaller than that recorded in the surrounding regions. 

Since the absolute magnitude of the SWE trends appears to increase toward the end of the snow cycle, as suggested by the 
progressive saturation of the color scale in Figures \ref{fig:swe_clima_trends_feb}--\ref{fig:swe_clima_trends_may} as the season

into \SI{50}{\meter}-wide elevation bands, and the mean SWE of each band was computed on a daily basis over the 1951--2023 period. 
This approach allowed for the estimation of trend magnitude for each combination of date and elevation band, yielding the results

\section{SCD predictions}

Equation \eqref{eq:scf_IT-SNOW} could not be used to directly convert snow water equivalent into snow cover estimates, since snow 
density isn't tracked by our modelling chain. However, following a similar approach to the one adopted for MODIS, in which every
pixel with more than half of its area covered by snow is labelled as snow-covered, it was possible to identify a SWE threshold 
separating bare ground from snowpack presence as
\begin{equation}
    \text{SWE}\,=\,5 \times 10^{-2} \rho_s,
    \label{eq:swe_thresh_scf}
\end{equation}
where the snow cover fraction SCF has already been set to 0.5. To attain a numerical estimate of this SWE threshold, assumptions on 
typical $\rho_s$ values had to be made. Considering that freshly fallen and ripe snow densities are expected to range between 50--250 
and 350--450 \SI{}{\kilo\gram\per\meter\cubed} respectively depending on both thermal and microphysical conditions, a value of 
$\SI{300}{\kilo\gram\per\meter\cubed}$ was adopted as representative of bulk snow properties. Substituing this density value into 
Equation \eqref{eq:swe_thresh_scf} yields a SWE threshold of \SI{15}{\milli\meter}, which was subsequently applied to generate 
daily snow cover binary maps. This made it possible to evaluate the duration of the snow season across the IH-GAR, revealing 
substantial spatial variability driven by the complex topography of the study area, with mountainous regions generally exhibiting 
more favorable conditions for the accurrence of cryospheric processes. This spatial pattern can be clearly identified in Figure 
\ref{fig:clima_scd_map_1991_2020}, which shows the 1991--2020 climatological SCD. Notably, the areas previously excluded from the 
SWE analysis are consistently associated with snow coverage all year round.

and the average climatological SCD of the corresponding \SI{5}{\meter}-wide elevation band it belongs to. Positive and negative 
deviations thus indicate areas characterized by longer or shorter snow cycles respectively compared to locations at similar elevations.
