\section{Model description}

The snow model developed during this thesis uses a raster based approach, in which adjacent pixels are treated as independent, thus neglecting every kind of 
horizontal transport. The main drivers of snowpack dynamics are total precipitation and air temperature, available on a daily basis. The whole code is 
available in Appendix \ref{app:model_impl}.

\subsection{Snow accumulation}

The simplest way to estimate daily snowfall when only air temperature and total precipitation are available is to compare the mean daily temperature with 
a threshold temperature $T_\tau$ in order to determine the precipitation phase. This approach, however, completely neglects the variability of air 
temperature within the day, and therefore yields only rough estimates. When daily maximum and minimum temperatures are also available, such as in this 
thesis, the fraction of precipitation that reaches the ground as snow can be evaluated more realistically, since the full daily temperature range is known. 
In particular, the snowfall $S_f$ at each grid point is evaluated as 
\begin{equation}
    S_{f} =
    \begin{cases}
        P
            & \hspace*{1.5em} T_{\max} < T_{\tau} \\[8pt]
        P \cdot \dfrac{T_{\tau} - T_{\min}}{T_{\max} - T_{\min}}
            & \hspace*{1.5em} T_{\min} < T_{\tau} < T_{\max} \\[8pt]
        0
            & \hspace*{1.5em} T_{\min} > T_{\tau},
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:phase_partitioning_SWE_model}
\end{equation}
where $T_{\max}$ and $T_{\min}$ are the daily maximum and minimum temperatures respectively, and $P$ the total daily precipitation.

\subsection{Snowmelt}

The snow models considered in Chapter \ref{cap:snow_products} had different approaches to snowmelt estimation. "J-snow", the modelling chain that led to 
the generation of the Po River District dataset, simulated the whole energy balance of the snowpack, whereas "S3M" treated the problem through a hybrid 
degree day method, focusing on both air temperature and shortwave radiation. Both methods, however, are not feasible to evaluate the snowpack dynamics 
over the 1951--2023 period, as they are data intensive requiring inputs that are unavailable over the whole investigated period.

The chosen modelling approach is thus a canonical degree-day method, in which air temperature is used to account for all the energy fluxes which alter the 
energy state of the snowpack. In particular, the model developed during this thesis follows the approach suggested by \cite{Magnusson_DDF}, thus computing 
the snowmelt at each grid point as 
\begin{equation}
    \text{M}\,=\,\text{DDF}\left\{\left(T_{mean}\,-\,T_{melt}\right)\,+\,m_m \cdot \ln\left[1\,+\,\exp\left(-\frac{T_{mean}\,-\,T_{melt}}{m_m}\right)\right]\right\},
    \label{eq:swe_melt}
\end{equation}
where $T_{mean}$ is the mean daily temperature, $T_{melt}$ is a threshold temperature for snowmelt to occur and $m_m$ is a smoothing factor measured in 
$\SI{}{\degreeCelsius}$. 

In Equation \eqref{eq:swe_melt} the term DDF stands for degree day factor, which is a parameter measured in 
$\SI{}{\milli\meter\per\degreeCelsius\per\day}$, and multiplied gives the amount of water melted away on a given day. In order to account for shortwave in 
some extent, and in order to keep track of its variability, which has a significant role in the snowpack energy balance, the DDF has an annual cycle with 
the following shape
\begin{equation}
    \text{DDF}_{i}\,=\,\text{DDF}_{ave}\,+\,\text{DDF}_{amp}\cdot\sin\left(\frac{2\pi \left(i\,-\,81\right)}{366}\right),
    \label{eq:swe_ddf}
\end{equation}
where the subscript $i$ is the number of the day in a solar year, $\text{DDF}_{ave}$ is the average value of the degree day factor, whereas $\text{DDF}_{amp}$ 
is the amplitude of its oscillation. The DDF peaks on summer sulstice, following the solar radial evolution. 

The term within curly brackets in Equation \eqref{eq:swe_melt} is dimensionally a temperature: the neperian logarithm is necessary to ensure a smooth 
transition at the threshold snowmelt temperature.

\subsection{SWE evaluation}

Finally, the temporal evolution of snow water equivalent over the investigated period is simulated by accumulating, on a daily basis, the difference 
between solid precipitation and snowmelt. The SWE on day $t$ is therefore computed at each pixel as 
\begin{equation}
    \text{SWE}(t)\,=\,\text{SWE}(t\,-\,1)\,+\,S_{f}(t)\,-\,M(t), 
    \label{eq:swe_swe}
\end{equation}
where $t$ or $t\,-\,1$ denote the day on which each quantity is evaluated. When the computed snowmelt exceeds the available snow water equivalent at a 
given location, the corresponding pixel value is set to zero, as this condition indicates the complete meltout of the snowpack. Before producing SWE maps 
for the IH-GAR territory, however, the model parameters $\left(T_{\tau},\,T_{melt},\,\text{DDF}_{ave},\,\text{DDF}_{amp},\,m_m \right)$ must be calibrated. 
The calibration procedure is described in the following chapter.
