\chapter{SWE model}
\label{cap:swe_model}

Le limitazioni dei prodotti nivologici esistenti, discusse nel Capitolo precedente, evidenziano la necessità di disporre di una ricostruzione nivologica 
di lunga durata e basata solamente su dati osservativi, di modo che possano essere effettuate delle analisi di trend robuste, senza essere influenzati da 
eventuali bias che tipicamente caratterizzano i dati di reanalisi. Per modellare nel modo più accurato possibile il manto nevoso, sarebbe necessario prendere 
in considerazione tutti i fenomeni fisici che ne caratterizzano la sua evoluzione e modifica durante la stagione nevosa, a partire da una stima di tutti i 
termini che costituiscono il bilancio energetico del manto nevoso, per arrivare a compattazione e percolazione dell'acqua all'interno della struttura porosa 
della neve, precedendo di fatto il runoff. La problematica principale che rende di fatto inapplicabile un approccio di questo genere è il fatto che richiede 
una quantità innumerevole di input, come per esempio la distribuzione della short wave radiation, mappe di velocità del vento, misure di temperatura del 
suolo che sono state effettuate su una rete densa di stazioni solamente dagli anni 2000 in poi. Per questo motivo, il modello che viene presentato in questo 
capitolo, presenta un approccio decisamente minimalista data la scarsità di variabili che possono essere utilizzate come input su un periodo lungo come 
quello dal 1951 al 2023. Infatti, per simulare le dinamiche del manto nevoso nell'ambito di questa tesi, vengono presi in considerazione solo dati di 
precipitazione totale e temperatura, che vengono di fatto utilizzati come soli driver delle dinamiche d'accumulo e di fusione della risorsa nivale. 

Il capitolo presenta in primo luogo i dataset meteorologici utilizzati come forcing e la procedura impiegata per la loro ricostruzione spaziale, per poi 
passare alla descrizione del modello vero e proprio. Il processo di calibrazione dello stesso, effettuato su serie osservative di spessore nevoso, è 
affrontato separatamente nel seguente capitolo.

The chapter follows the workflow of the proposed approach. The gridded datasets used as model forcing are presented first, followed by a description 
of precipitation phase partitioning and of the computation of snowmelt and SWE. Model calibration is addressed separately in Chapter \ref{cap:swe_calibration}.



\section{Model inputs}

The gridded temperature and precipitation datasets, used as model inputs, were both developed by the Department of Environmental Science and Policy of the 
University of Milan, building on earlier work aimed at rescuing new station records and improving the quality of existing ones. The computational chain 
used to generate these rasters is based on the anomaly method, which assumes that the spatio-temporal structure of a meteorological signal can be 
described as the superposition of two fields, namely being the climatological normals over a given reference period and the departures from them. In the 
following, only the procedure for precipitation is presented. For temperature, the only difference lies in the type of anomalies considered, which are 
additive rather than multiplicative.



\subsection{Gridding procedure}

The gridding procedure starts from a spatially dense and temporally continuous dataset of station observations, which has been quality checked for spikes 
and missing values to prevent systematic errors from propagating into the gridded fields. The three main steps to generate daily precipitation maps are:
\begin{itemize}[label=$\blacktriangleright$]
    \item \textit{calculation of high-resolution climatologies}, which are required as a reference standpoint for daily series reconstruction. The first 
    step to obtain gridded climatologies was the computation of precipitation normals over the 1991--2020 period for each station of the precipitation 
    dataset. The method selected to generate the climatologies rasters is the Local Weighted Linear Regression (LWLR) of precipitation vs elevation, 
    which already proved to be a robust method to reconstruct gridded maps \parencite{LWLR_Crespi}. The weights for the evaluation of the regression 
    coefficients consider the distances and the level of orographic similarity between the stations and a specific grid-point. In particular, the weight 
    for the i-th station involved in the LWLR for grid point $\left(x,\,y\right)$ is:
    \begin{equation}
        w_i \left(x,\,y\right)\,=\,w_i^{rad}\left(x,\,y\right) \cdot w_i^{h}\left(x,\,y\right) \cdot w_i^{st}\left(x,\,y\right) \cdot w_i^{facet}\left(x,\,y\right) \cdot w_i^{dsea}\left(x,\,y\right),
        \label{eq:weights_linear_regression}
    \end{equation}  
    where the superscript correspond respectively to radial position, elevation, slope steepness, slope orientation and distance from the sea. The single 
    factors in Equation \eqref{eq:weights_linear_regression} are based on gaussian functions, so that 
    \begin{equation}
        w_i^{par} \left(x,\,y\right)\,=\,\exp{-\frac{\left[\Delta_i^{par}\left(x,\,y\right)\right]^2}{c^{par}}},
        \label{eq:gaussian_form_weight}
    \end{equation}
    where $\Delta_i^{par}$ is the difference between the station and the grid point features and $c^{par}$ determines the width of the gaussian. The $c^{par}$ 
    factors are locally optimized to maximize the agreement between estimated and observed normals. At a specific location $\left(x,\,y\right)$ the 
    monthly normals are thus reconstructed as
    \begin{equation}
        P_{clima}\left(x,\,y\right)\,=\,q\left(x,\,y\right)\,+\,m\left(x,\,y\right) \cdot h\left(x,\,y\right),
        \label{eq:clima_precipitation_gridding}
    \end{equation}
    where $P_{clima}$ is the computed normal, $q$ and $m$ are the site-specific regression coefficients and $h$ the elevation of the considered grid point. 
    The regression coefficients for each grid point are estimated taking into account the \num{30} stations with the highest weights. Furthermore, to attain 
    robust regression coefficients estimates, the LWLR was performed \num{30} times by iteratively removing each station: if the difference between the 
    lowest and highest estimates exceeded \num{30} \% of the weighted mean obtained considering all 30 stations, the $c^{par}$ where relaxed by \num{10} \% 
    in order to avoid weighting too much some stations.
    \item \textit{computation of monthly precipitation maps}, which requires monthly anomaly maps to compute the actual monthly values over the whole 
    investigated period as 
    \begin{equation}
        P_{month}\left(x,\,y\right)\,=\,P_{clima}\left(x,\,y\right) \cdot A_{month}\left(x,\,y\right),
        \label{eq:monthly_precipitation_gridding}
    \end{equation}
    where $ P_{month}$ and $A_{month}$ are the precipitation totals and the multiplicative anomaly for a given month at a specific grid point 
    $\left(x,\,y\right)$. To generate the anomaly maps, all the series included in the station dataset are transformed into anomaly series with respect 
    to the 1991-2020 monthly normals. Each monthly anomaly value for each grid point is then obtained as a weighted mean of the values of the \num{10} 
    closest stations: the weights take into consideration the distance, difference in elevation and angular distribution with respect to the grid point 
    to be reconstructed. The distance weight, of gaussian form as the one in Equation , gets halved at the distance of the third closest station, whereas 
    the elevation one is halved at \num{800} meters for pixels below $\SI{1600}{\meter}$ a.s.l. or at half the elevation of the grid point otherwise. This 
    peculiar scaling approach allows for weights to decrease more gradually where fewer stations are available, but avoids estrapolation from more distant 
    stations whenever there is high data availability. 
    \item \textit{estimation of daily precipitation values}, which at a specific location $\left(x,\,y\right)$ is computed as 
    \begin{equation}
        P\left(x,\,y\right)\,=\,P_{month}\left(x,\,y\right) \cdot F_{day}\left(x,\,y\right),
        \label{eq:monthly_precipitation_gridding}
    \end{equation}
    where $P$ is the daily precipitation value and $F_{day}\left(x,\,y\right)$ is the ratio between the daily precipitation and the corresponding monthly 
    total. In order to obtain $F_{day}\left(x,\,y\right)$ at each grid point, the station series were transformed into daily fractions of precipitation 
    with respect to the corresponding monthly total. This values were later interpolated over the spatial grid using the same method presented in the 
    previous bulletpoint.
\end{itemize}



\subsection{Precipitation data}

The generation of daily precipitation grids for the 1951--2023 requires a dense network of station-based time series that have undergone rigorous 
quality control to identify and correct potential reading errors, as well as to account for missing observations. A major challenge in establishing 
such a dataset over the IH-GAR is the substantial spatial and temporal heterogeneity of meteorological observations in Italy. Following the 
dissolution of the Italian Hydrographic Service in the 1990s, responsibility for the management and maintenance of meteorological networks was 
transferred to various regional authorities. This institutional fragmentation has resulted in considerable inconsistencies in the continuity, 
availability, and temporal homogeneity of observational records, especially because it happened exactly when automatic weather stations were 
being introduced in the monitoring network. The first step toward the generation of daily precipitation grids by \cite{precipitation_IHGAR} 
was thus to recover new Italian rainfall data and further enhance existing ones, in order to have as much series as possible to accurately reconstruct 
precipitation maps. Substantial effort was put into a deep reorganization of the observations, as well as in merging attempt between manual and 
automatic series, in order to enhance the temporal coverage of precipitation readings. The resulting station dataset, consisting of series with at 
least \num{1000} daily observations, was composed by \num{8337} stations with daily data and including station that are placed outside the IH-GAR 
borders, in order to ensure that every grid point is surrounded by a significant amount of weather stations.

\section{Model structure}

The snow model developed during this thesis uses a raster based approach, in which adjacent pixels are treated as independent, thus neglecting every kind of 
horizontal transport. The main drivers of snowpack dynamics are total precipitation and air temperature, available on a daily basis. The whole code is 
available in Appendix \ref{app:model_impl}.



\subsection{Snow accumulation}

The simplest way to estimate daily snowfall when only air temperature and total precipitation are available is to compare the mean daily temperature with 
a threshold temperature $T_\tau$ in order to determine the precipitation phase. This approach, however, completely neglects the variability of air 
temperature within the day, and therefore yields only rough estimates. When daily maximum and minimum temperatures are also available, such as in this 
thesis, the fraction of precipitation that reaches the ground as snow can be evaluated more realistically, since the full daily temperature range is known. 
In particular, the snowfall $S_f$ at each grid point is evaluated as 
\begin{equation}
    S_{f} =
    \begin{cases}
        P
            & \hspace*{1.5em} T_{\max} < T_{\tau} \\[8pt]
        P \cdot \dfrac{T_{\tau} - T_{\min}}{T_{\max} - T_{\min}}
            & \hspace*{1.5em} T_{\min} < T_{\tau} < T_{\max} \\[8pt]
        0
            & \hspace*{1.5em} T_{\min} > T_{\tau},
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:phase_partitioning_SWE_model}
\end{equation}
where $T_{\max}$ and $T_{\min}$ are the daily maximum and minimum temperatures respectively, and $P$ the total daily precipitation.



\subsection{Snowmelt}

The snow models discussed in Chapter \ref{cap:snow_products} adopted different approaches to snowmelt estimation. "J-snow", the modelling chain that led 
to the generation of the Po River District dataset, explicitly simulated the energy balance of the snowpack, whereas "S3M" employed an hybrid degree-day 
method, accounting for both air temperature and shortwave radiation. Neither method, however, is suitable for investigating snowpack dynamics over the 
1951--2023 period, as both require input variables that are not unavailable continuously throughout the entire study period.

The modelling approach adopted is therefore a canonical degree-day method, in which mean air temperature is used as a proxy for the various energy fluxes 
affecting the energy state of the snowpack. In particular, the model follows the formulation proposed by \cite{Magnusson_DDF}, whereby the daily snowmelt 
at each grid point is computed as 
\begin{equation}
    \text{M}\,=\,\text{DDF}\left\{\left(T_{mean}\,-\,T_{melt}\right)\,+\,m_m \cdot \ln\left[1\,+\,\exp\left(-\frac{T_{mean}\,-\,T_{melt}}{m_m}\right)\right]\right\},
    \label{eq:swe_melt}
\end{equation}
where $T_{mean}$ is the mean daily temperature, $T_{melt}$ is the threshold snowmelt temperature, and $m_m$ is a smoothing parameter measured in 
$\SI{}{\degreeCelsius}$. 

In Equation \eqref{eq:swe_melt},  DDF denotes the degree-day factor, which is a parameter measured in $\SI{}{\milli\meter\per\degreeCelsius\per\day}$: 
its multiplication by the temperature dependent term yields the amount of snow melted away on a given day. To account, at least partially, for the seasonal 
variability of shortwave radiation, which plays a significant role in the snowpack energy balance, the degree-day factor is prescribed with an annual cycle 
according to
\begin{equation}
    \text{DDF}_{t}\,=\,\text{DDF}_{ave}\,+\,\text{DDF}_{amp}\cdot\sin\left(\frac{2\pi \left(t\,-\,81\right)}{366}\right),
    \label{eq:swe_ddf}
\end{equation}
where $t$ is the day of the solar year, $\text{DDF}_{ave}$ is the mean degree day factor, whereas $\text{DDF}_{amp}$ is the amplitude of its oscillation. 
With this formulation, the DDF peaks on summer solstice, in qualitative agreement with the seasonal evolution of incoming solar radiation. 

The term enclosed in curly brackets in Equation \eqref{eq:swe_melt} has the dimensions of a temperature: the natural logarithm ensures a smooth transition 
between melting and freezing conditions around the snowmelt threshold.



\subsection{SWE evaluation}

Finally, the temporal evolution of snow water equivalent over the investigated period is simulated by accumulating, on a daily basis, the difference 
between solid precipitation and snowmelt. The SWE on day $t$ is therefore computed at each pixel as 
\begin{equation}
    \text{SWE}(t)\,=\,\text{SWE}(t\,-\,1)\,+\,S_{f}(t)\,-\,M(t), 
    \label{eq:swe_swe}
\end{equation}
where $t$ or $t\,-\,1$ denote the day on which each quantity is evaluated. When the computed snowmelt exceeds the available snow water equivalent at a 
given location, the corresponding pixel value is set to zero, as this condition indicates the complete meltout of the snowpack. Before producing SWE maps 
for the IH-GAR territory, however, the model parameters $\left(T_{\tau},\,T_{melt},\,\text{DDF}_{ave},\,\text{DDF}_{amp},\,m_m \right)$ must be calibrated. 
The calibration procedure is described in the following chapter.
