\chapter{SWE model}
\label{cap:swe_model}

The limitations of existing snow products, discussed in the previous Chapter, highlight the need for a long term snow reconstruction over IH-GAR based 
exclusively on observational data. To model the snowpack as accurately as possible, 
it would be necessary to take into account all the physical processes that characterize its evolution throughout the snow season, starting from an 
estimate of all terms contributing to the snowpack energy balance to then focus on compaction and water percolation, which ultimately precedes runoff. 
The main issue that makes such an approach effectively inapplicable within the framework of this thesis is that it requires a wide variety of input 
variables, such as the spatial distribution of incoming shortwave radiation, wind speed maps and soil temperature measurements, which have only been 
collected through a spatially dense station network since the 2000s. For this reason, the model presented in this chapter adopts a substantially 
minimalist approach, given the limited number of meteorological inputs available over a period as long as 1951--2023. Indeed, to simulate snowpack 
dynamics, only temperature and precipitation data are used: these two variables are therefore the only drivers of snow accumulation and snowmelt.

In the following sections, the temperature and precipitation datasets, as well as the procedure adopted for their spatial reconstruction, are presented 
first. The second half of this chapter is instead dedicated to the actual description of the snow model. The model calibration process, carried out 
comparing model outputs to observational snow depth time series, is addressed separately in Chapter \ref{cap:swe_calibration}.










\section{Model inputs}

The gridded temperature and precipitation datasets, used as model inputs, have both a spatial resolution of \num{30} arc-seconds. They were developed 
by the Department of Environmental Science and Policy of the University of Milan, building on earlier work aimed at rescuing new station records and 
improving the quality of existing ones. The numerical procedure chain used to generate these rasters is based on the anomaly method \parencite{precipitation_IHGAR}, 
which assumes that the spatio-temporal structure of a meteorological signal can be described as the superposition of two fields, namely being the 
climatological normals over a reference period of thirty years and the departures from them. In the following, only the precipitation version is presented. 
For temperature, the only difference is that are used additive anomalies rather than multiplicative ones.



\subsection{Gridding procedure}

The gridding procedure used to produce the precipitation dataset requires a spatially dense and temporally continuous dataset of station observations, 
which has undergone rigorous quality checks to identify spikes and missing values. Such control is essential for preventing systematic errors from 
propagating into the gridded fields. This method is composed by three main steps, namely:
\begin{itemize}[label=$\blacktriangleright$]
    \item \textit{calculation of high-resolution climatologies}, which are required as a reference for the reconstruction of daily series. The first 
    step in deriving the gridded climatologies consisted in computing the 1991--2020 precipitation normals for each station of the dataset. The method 
    used to generate the climatological rasters is the Local Weighted Linear Regression (LWLR) of precipitation against elevation, which had already 
    proven to be a robust approach for reconstructing gridded fields \parencite{LWLR_Crespi}. At a specific location $\left(x,\,y\right)$, the 
    monthly normals were computed as
    \begin{equation}
        P_{clima}\left(x,\,y\right)\,=\,q\left(x,\,y\right)\,+\,m\left(x,\,y\right) \cdot h\left(x,\,y\right),
        \label{eq:clima_precipitation_gridding}
    \end{equation}
    where $P_{clima}$ is the reconstructed normal, $q$ and $m$ are the location-specific regression coefficients, and $h$ the elevation of the considered 
    grid point. The weights used to evaluate the regression coefficients account for both the distances and the level of orographic similarity between 
    each station and the target grid point. In particular, the weight assigned to the i-th station for a grid point $\left(x,\,y\right)$ was given by
    \begin{equation}
        w_i \left(x,\,y\right)\,=\,w_i^{rad}\left(x,\,y\right) \cdot w_i^{h}\left(x,\,y\right) \cdot w_i^{st}\left(x,\,y\right) \cdot w_i^{facet}\left(x,\,y\right) \cdot w_i^{dsea}\left(x,\,y\right),
        \label{eq:weights_linear_regression}
    \end{equation}  
    where the superscripts refer, respectively, to radial distance, elevation, slope steepness, slope orientation, and distance from the sea \parencite{precipitation_IHGAR}. 
    Each factor on the right side of Equation \eqref{eq:weights_linear_regression} was defined through a Gaussian function, so that 
    \begin{equation}
        w_i^{par} \left(x,\,y\right)\,=\,\exp{-\frac{\left[\Delta_i^{par}\left(x,\,y\right)\right]^2}{c^{par}}},
        \label{eq:gaussian_form_weight}
    \end{equation}
    where $\Delta_i^{par}$ represents the difference in features between the selected station and the specific grid point, while $c^{par}$ controls the width of the 
    Gaussian. The $c^{par}$ parameters were locally optimized to maximize the agreement between estimated and observed normals. For each grid point, the 
    regression coefficients were estimated taking into account the \num{30} stations with the highest weights. Furthermore, to improve the robustness 
    of the estimates, the LWLR was performed \num{30} times in a leave-one-out framework, removing one station at each iteration. If the difference 
    between the lowest and highest estimates exceeded \num{30} \% of the weighted mean obtained using all 30 stations, the $c^{par}$ were relaxed by 
    \num{10} \% to prevent individual stations to dictate the normal values \parencite{precipitation_IHGAR}.
    \item \textit{computation of monthly precipitation maps}, which relies on monthly anomaly maps to reconstruct the actual monthly totals over the 
    entire investigated period as 
    \begin{equation}
        P_{month}\left(x,\,y\right)\,=\,P_{clima}\left(x,\,y\right) \cdot A_{month}\left(x,\,y\right),
        \label{eq:monthly_precipitation_gridding}
    \end{equation}
    where $ P_{month}$ and $A_{month}$ are, respectively, the total precipitation and the multiplicative anomaly for a given month at the grid point 
    $\left(x,\,y\right)$. To generate the anomaly maps, all station series were converted into anomaly series relative to the 1991--2020 monthly normals. 
    The departures from the climatologies were then computed at each grid point as a weighted mean of the values from the \num{10} nearest stations 
    \parencite{precipitation_IHGAR}. The weights accounted for distance, elevation difference, and the angular distribution of the stations around the 
    grid point being reconstructed. The distance weight, which had a Gaussian form as in Equation \eqref{eq:gaussian_form_weight}, was halved at the 
    distance of the third closest station. The elevation one, on the other hands, got halved at an elevation difference of \num{800} meters for grid 
    points below $\SI{1600}{\meter}$ a.s.l. or at half the elevation of the grid point otherwise \parencite{precipitation_IHGAR}. This adaptive scaling 
    scheme allowed the weights to decrease more gradually in areas with a lower station density, while limiting the influence of less compatible stations 
    whenever there was high data availability.
    \item \textit{estimation of daily precipitation values}, which, at a given location $\left(x,\,y\right)$, were computed as 
    \begin{equation}
        P\left(x,\,y\right)\,=\,P_{month}\left(x,\,y\right) \cdot F_{day}\left(x,\,y\right),
        \label{eq:daily_precipitation_gridding}
    \end{equation}
    where $P$ denotes the daily precipitation value and $F_{day}\left(x,\,y\right)$ represents the ratio between the daily precipitation and the 
    corresponding monthly total. To obtain $F_{day}\left(x,\,y\right)$ at each grid point, the station time series were first converted into daily 
    precipitation fractions by dividing each daily value by the corresponding monthly one. The resulting fractions were then interpolated over the 
    spatial grid using the same method described in the previous bullet point \parencite{precipitation_IHGAR}.
\end{itemize}



\subsection{Precipitation data}

The gridded precipitation dataset used as model input in this thesis is publically available through the \href{https://dataverse.unimi.it/dataset.xhtml?persistentId=doi:10.13130/RD_UNIMI/CSYYAP}{UNIMI Dataverse}.
Over the IH-GAR, the dataset indicates an average annual precipitation of $1090\,\pm\,190\,\SI{}{\milli\meter}$ for the 1991--2020 period. This spatial 
average, however, masks substantial heterogeneity in the annual precipitation climatology, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:annual_prec_clima_1991_2020}. 
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering

    \caption{Annual precipitation climatology for the Italian Hydrological part of the Greater Alpine Arc (IH-GAR), computed over the 1991--2020

    lifting.}
    \label{fig:annual_prec_clima_1991_2020}

The highest totals are indeed observed at the foothills of the Alps and in the Northern Apennines, mainly as a result of the stronger orographic uplift 
of air masses. Conversely, the lowest values occur in the Po River Delta and in the lowlands of the innermost Alpine valleys, such as the Aosta and Venosta 
ones. The timing of precipitation maxima is site-specific, as different climatic regimes are present in the study region. In the lowlands, precipitations 
are typically higher during spring and autumn, whereas the inner Alpine regions experience a single peak during summer. The transition zone, located along 
the foothills of the main mountain ranges, is instead characterized by a markedly wetter autumn season. In particular, close to the border between Italy 
and Slovenia, cumulative precipitation can exceed $\SI{1000}{\milli\meter}$ over the course of these three months.

\subsection{Temperature data}

The paper describing the generation of these temperature rasters is currently under review: the data will be made publicly available through the Unimi 
Dataverse once the study has been accepted for publication. Three temperature fields were reconstructed on a daily basis, namely mean, minimum and maximum 
temperature. 

As expected, the temperature dataset indicates that the IH-GAR has experienced marked changes in response to global warming. However, this trends is not 
characterized by a steady increase over the 1951--2023 period, but rather by a pronounced shift in behaviour in the late 1970s, as shown in Figure 
\ref{fig:mean_temperature_trend}. This is consistent with the broader European climate evolution: until the mid-70s, most temperature related indices 
exhibited either statistically non-significant cooling trends or approximately stationary long-term conditions, with departures from climatology mainly 
associated with interannual variability \parencite{two_step_warming_europe}.

    \centering

    \caption{Mean daily temperature anomaly series with respect to the 1951-1980 climatology over the IH-GAR. The 4 month and 40 month running means are

\section{Model structure}

The snow model developed during this thesis uses a raster based approach, in which adjacent pixels are treated as independent, thus neglecting every kind of 
horizontal transport. The main drivers of snowpack dynamics are total precipitation and air temperature, available on a daily basis. The whole code is 
available in Appendix \ref{app:model_impl}.



\subsection{Snow accumulation}

The simplest way to estimate daily snowfall when only air temperature and total precipitation are available is to compare the mean daily temperature with 
a threshold temperature $T_\tau$ in order to determine the precipitation phase. This approach, however, completely neglects the variability of air 
temperature within the day, and therefore yields only rough estimates. When daily maximum and minimum temperatures are also available, such as in this 
thesis, the fraction of precipitation that reaches the ground as snow can be evaluated more realistically, since the full daily temperature range is known. 
In particular, the snowfall $S_f$ at each grid point is evaluated as 
\begin{equation}
    S_{f} =
    \begin{cases}
        P
            & \hspace*{1.5em} T_{\max} < T_{\tau} \\[8pt]
        P \cdot \dfrac{T_{\tau} - T_{\min}}{T_{\max} - T_{\min}}
            & \hspace*{1.5em} T_{\min} < T_{\tau} < T_{\max} \\[8pt]
        0
            & \hspace*{1.5em} T_{\min} > T_{\tau},
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:phase_partitioning_SWE_model}
\end{equation}
where $T_{\max}$ and $T_{\min}$ are the daily maximum and minimum temperatures respectively, and $P$ the total daily precipitation.



\subsection{Snowmelt}

The snow models discussed in Chapter \ref{cap:snow_products} adopted different approaches to snowmelt estimation. "J-snow", the modelling chain that led 
to the generation of the Po River District dataset, explicitly simulated the energy balance of the snowpack, whereas "S3M" employed an hybrid degree-day 
method, accounting for both air temperature and shortwave radiation. Neither method, however, is suitable for investigating snowpack dynamics over the 
1951--2023 period, as both require input variables that are not unavailable continuously throughout the entire study period.

The modelling approach adopted is therefore a canonical degree-day method, in which mean air temperature is used as a proxy for the various energy fluxes 
affecting the energy state of the snowpack. In particular, the model follows the formulation proposed by \cite{Magnusson_DDF}, whereby the daily snowmelt 
at each grid point is computed as 
\begin{equation}
    \text{M}\,=\,\text{DDF}\left\{\left(T_{mean}\,-\,T_{melt}\right)\,+\,m_m \cdot \ln\left[1\,+\,\exp\left(-\frac{T_{mean}\,-\,T_{melt}}{m_m}\right)\right]\right\},
    \label{eq:swe_melt}
\end{equation}
where $T_{mean}$ is the mean daily temperature, $T_{melt}$ is the threshold snowmelt temperature, and $m_m$ is a smoothing parameter measured in 
$\SI{}{\degreeCelsius}$. 

In Equation \eqref{eq:swe_melt},  DDF denotes the degree-day factor, which is a parameter measured in $\SI{}{\milli\meter\per\degreeCelsius\per\day}$: 
its multiplication by the temperature dependent term yields the amount of snow melted away on a given day. To account, at least partially, for the seasonal 
variability of shortwave radiation, which plays a significant role in the snowpack energy balance, the degree-day factor is prescribed with an annual cycle 
according to
\begin{equation}
    \text{DDF}_{t}\,=\,\text{DDF}_{ave}\,+\,\text{DDF}_{amp}\cdot\sin\left(\frac{2\pi \left(t\,-\,81\right)}{366}\right),
    \label{eq:swe_ddf}
\end{equation}
where $t$ is the day of the solar year, $\text{DDF}_{ave}$ is the mean degree day factor, whereas $\text{DDF}_{amp}$ is the amplitude of its oscillation. 
With this formulation, the DDF peaks on summer solstice, in qualitative agreement with the seasonal evolution of incoming solar radiation. 

The term enclosed in curly brackets in Equation \eqref{eq:swe_melt} has the dimensions of a temperature: the natural logarithm ensures a smooth transition 
between melting and freezing conditions around the snowmelt threshold.



\subsection{SWE evaluation}

Finally, the temporal evolution of snow water equivalent over the investigated period is simulated by accumulating, on a daily basis, the difference 
between solid precipitation and snowmelt. The SWE on day $t$ is therefore computed at each pixel as 
\begin{equation}
    \text{SWE}(t)\,=\,\text{SWE}(t\,-\,1)\,+\,S_{f}(t)\,-\,M(t), 
    \label{eq:swe_swe}
\end{equation}
where $t$ or $t\,-\,1$ denote the day on which each quantity is evaluated. When the computed snowmelt exceeds the available snow water equivalent at a 
given location, the corresponding pixel value is set to zero, as this condition indicates the complete meltout of the snowpack. Before producing SWE maps 
for the IH-GAR territory, however, the model parameters $\left(T_{\tau},\,T_{melt},\,\text{DDF}_{ave},\,\text{DDF}_{amp},\,m_m \right)$ must be calibrated. 
The calibration procedure is described in the following chapter.
