\chapter{Introduction}
\label{cap:introduction}

In the last few decades, Europe has emerged as a hotspot for snow droughts, i.e. periods of persistently below average snowpack, 
which have become both more frequent and longer-lasting since the 1990s. In the Italian Alps, for instance, snow water storage 
reached record lows during both the 2022 and 2023 winters, resulting in a reduction of snowmelt runoff substantially altering 
the hydrological regime of rivers draining mountainous terrain. To investigate this phenomenon, \cite{snow_drought} analyzed 
historical snow depth records collected between 1930 and 2023 from \num{15} weather stations distributed across Northern Italy. 
As shown in Figure \ref{fig:winter_anomalies_colombo}, their study indicated that the incrising frequency and severity of snow

1990s are a consequence of warmer and drier winters relative to the 1961--1990 climatological mean. These findings are 
consistent with the Alpine wide assessment conducted by \cite{Matiu_HS}, based on the analysis of more than 800 snow depth time 
series, which highlighted a shift toward snow scarce winters starting from the late 1980s. Moreover, a decline in seasonal mean 
snow depth of 8.4\% per decade between 1971 and 2019 was detected as a consequence of the ongoing global warming. Both studies, 
however, rely solely on manual station networks, which are typically too sparse and markedly biased toward low to mid elevations, to 
accurately represent the strong spatial variability of snow cover in complex mountainous terrain. To this end, numerical modelling 
and remote sensing techniques have been employed to provide spatialized estimates of snowpack properties over a specific study area. 
At the time of writing, three main snow products are available over Northern Italy, each of them with a spatial resolution of 
approximately \SI{500}{\meter}: 
\begin{itemize}[label=$\blacktriangleright$]
    \item \textit{MODIS}, a snow dataset derived from satellite imagery that provides daily binary maps for the entire Italian peninsula 
    from 24 February 2000 to 30 September 2025. Specifically, a value of one denotes a snow covered pixel, whereas zero is assigned 
    to bare ground conditions.
    \item \textit{IT-SNOW}, a snow reanalysis covering the entire Italian territory. It provides daily maps of snow water equivalent 
    (i.e. the amount of water stored as snow), snow depth and snow density, spanning the period from 1 September 2010 to 31 August 2025.
    \item \textit{Po River District dataset}, which focuses on the mountainous areas within the Po River District. It is a 
    hydrologically oriented reconstruction focusing on the snow season (3 October to 1 July) of each hydrological year between 
    1992 and 2021.
\end{itemize}
Although each of these snow products provides useful insights on snow cover dynamics across the Italian Alps, neither of them is 
suitable for robust trend estimates, since the spanned periods are too limited to avoid substantial influence by interannual 
variability. Furthermore, the Po River District modelling chain, which was used to generate the only snow product with climatological 
coverage (i.e. 30 year time window), integrates as inputs ERA5 precipitations, which are known to overestimate the actual values 
over mountainous terrain \parencite{ERA5_bias}. 

At the time of writing, these limitations highlight the need for a new spatially distributed dataset with sufficiently long 
temporal coverage to reliably assess the impacts of climate change on snowpack dynamics in Northern Italy. In 
particular, only historically recorded data should be used as meteorological forcing, to ensure the accuracy  
of the reconstruction. This thesis aims to address this gap by developing a modelling chain capable of simulating the seasonal snowpack 
between 1951 and 2023, providing new insights in snow cover changes and dynamics over the last \num{70} years.








\section{Thesis outline}

This thesis is structured as follows:
\begin{itemize}[label=$\blacktriangleright$]
    \item \textit{Chapter \ref{cap:introduction}} introduces the physical problem under investigation and the context in which it arises.
    \item \textit{Chapter \ref{cap:snow_basics}} provides the theoretical background needed to understand the work presented in this thesis. The 
    first part focuses on snow physics. It begins with the nucleation of ice crystals in cold clouds and follows the snowflakes down to the 
    ground, where they accumulate to form a new layer. It then describes snow metamorphism and the energy balance of the snowpack, and finally 
    liquid water percolation and runoff generation. The second part reviews snow measurement techniques, distinguishing between manual observations, 
    automatic monitoring networks and remote sensing, and discussing the strengths and limitations of each approach. The third part examines how 
    climate change is affecting snow cover, outlining the current state of snow dynamics worldwide.
    \item \textit{Chapter \ref{cap:snow_products}} introduces the study area and the three main snow products that cover it. It opens with a 
    description of the Italian hydrological portion of the Greater Alpine Region (GAR), which encompasses the Italian Alps and the Northern Apennines. 
    Each dataset is examined in turn, with particular attention to how it is generated and the estimates it provides over Italy. The chapter closes 
    with a comparison of the three snow products, highlighting the discrepancies between their estimates and thereby motivating the need for a new 
    approach to snow monitoring at the national scale.
    \item \textit{Chapter \ref{cap:swe_model}} describes the model used to generate the snow water equivalent (SWE) dataset. The chapter is 
    structured to closely follow the modelling workflow. First, the precipitation and air temperature datasets used as model inputs are introduced, 
    focusing on the numerical methods employed to generate them and discussing some preliminary results, as they may significantly influence the 
    spatial and temporal distribution of snow cover. The precipitation phase partitioning scheme and the approach used to estimate snowmelt are then 
    described. The chapter closes with the description of the procedure used to generate the SWE maps.
    \item \textit{Chapter \ref{cap:swe_calibration}} focuses on the calibration of the degree-day snow model using snow depth (HS) observations 
    collected from meteorological stations distributed across the Alps. The first section describes the quality control assessments and correction 
    procedure applied to station metadata and HS time series to improve data reliability. The chapter then introduces the calibration methodology, 
    including the underlying assumptions and the definition of the loss function. Finally, the calibration results are presented and discussed, with 
    particular attention to model performance across different months and elevation ranges.
    \item \textit{Chapter \ref{cap:results}} illustrates the main results of this thesis, focusing on both snow water equivalent (SWE) and snow 
    cover duration (SCD) metrics. The chapter opens with a discussion on glaciated areas, which must be masked to make reliable estimates 
    of seasonal snowpack properties. The second section deals with the amount of water stored as snow within the study area, making both temporal 
    trend estimates and comparisons with the available snow products. The last section describes a method to convert snow water equivalent maps into 
    binary snow cover ones, allowing for the evaluation of changes in the lenght of the snow season.
    \item \textit{Chapter \ref{cap:conclusions}} provides a summary of the main findings and discusses the limitations of this work, as well as 
    potential directions for future research.  
\end{itemize}
