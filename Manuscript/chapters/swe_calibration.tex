\section{HS data}

The snow depth dataset used to calibrate the degree-day model presented in the previous chapter was generated through a data rescue activity. It combines 
HS time series from \cite{Matiu_HS} with data provided by national and regional meteorological and hydrological services, including ARPA Piemonte, Centro 
Funzionale della Valle d'Aosta, Meteoswiss, WSL Institute for Snow and Avalanche Research SLF, ARPA Lombardia, Meteotrentino, ARPA Veneto, and the Austrian 
Hydrological Service \parencite{hs_data}. The final dataset includes 1052 stations, mostly automatic, distributed across the entire Alpine Arc, as shown 
in Figure \ref{fig:hs_series_origin}. Depending on the station, these time series can extend back to the 1960s, although most observations date from 1990 
onwards.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{swe_calibration/hs_series_origin.jpg}
    \caption{Spatial distribution of the stations that made up the original dataset. Each station is marked with the symbol associated with the 
    data source from which the data were obtained. Image from \cite{hs_data}.}
    \label{fig:hs_series_origin}
\end{figure}

The original HS dataset, however, presented several issues that needed to be addressed before it could be considered suitable for further analysis. The 
first reliability check that was done focused on the accuracy of the station metadata, specifically longitude, latitude and elevation. To this end, the 
reported elevation of each station was compared with that of the nearest pixel in the ALOS AW3D30 digital elevation model (DEM). A compatibility threshold 
of $\SI{12}{\meter}$ was adopted. Although this criterion is relatively strict given the complex topography of the Alpine Arc, it was considered 
appropriate due to the high spatial resolution of the DEM, namely $\SI{30}{\meter}$. Based on this threshold, more than half of the stations were 
classified as unreliable, as show in Figure \ref{fig:station_distribution_original}.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{swe_calibration/station_distribution_original.png}
    \caption{(a) Spatial distribution of stations with reliable (unreliable) metadata. (b) Frequency histogram of the differences between the reported 
    station elevations and those derived from the ALOS AW3D30 DEM. In both panels, green represents reliable stations, for which the absolute elevation 
    difference didn't exceed $\SI{12}{\meter}$, while red indicates stations that didn't meet this criterion.}
    \label{fig:station_distribution_original}
\end{figure}
Moreover, a second quality check revealed that not only the station metadata, but also the snow depth time series required further processing. In fact, to 
investigate potential issues within the snow depth dataset, HS series were compared with MODIS observations for stations falling within the study area 
and the period covered by \cite{MODIS_ITA}. For each station in this subset, the MODIS time series was extracted from the nearest grid point, while the HS 
data were converted into binary snow cover series using a threshold of $\SI{0}{\centi\meter}$: positive snow depth values were assigned a value of one, 
whereas zeros remained unchanged. Both time series were then organized in pseudo-hydrological years, extending from 1 September of a given year to 31 
August of the following one, to allow for a direct comparison. A perfect match was not expected, as station measurements are collected in-situ, whereas 
MODIS provides spatially distributed observations with pixels classified as snow free if the snow cover fraction does not exceed \num{0.5}. Nevertheless, 
the comparison shown in Figure \ref{fig:comparison_HS_MODIS_original} revealed substantial discrepancies between the two datasets.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{swe_calibration/comparison_HS_MODIS_original.png}
    \caption{(a) Comparison between SCD values computed from HS series and MODIS data. Each dot represents a pseudo-hydrological year, which is the 
    period spanning from 1 September of a given year to 31 August of the following one. Blue dots are data retrieved from reliable stations, whereas 
    orange ones are not. (b) Confusion matrix comparing snow cover observations according to MODIS and HS data.}
    \label{fig:comparison_HS_MODIS_original}
\end{figure}
In particular, the comparison between MODIS and HS time series showed a considerable scatter in SCD values, as visible in panel (a) of Figure 
\ref{fig:comparison_HS_MODIS_original}. Two distinct groups of data point deviating markedly from the identity line can indeed be identified: the first 
is concentrated in the upper-left corner of the plot, while the second one lies much closer to the x-axis than expected. The latter group mainly consists 
of hydrological years characterized by numerous and/or prolonged gaps in the snow depth records, with observations missing for much of the snow season. 
A representative example is shown in Figure \ref{fig:hs_series_with_gap}.  

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{swe_calibration/hs_series_with_gap.png}
    \caption{Example of an HS time series affected by extensive data gaps (highlighted in green). This series cannot be reliably reconstructed using 
    standard gap-filling methods because the SOD (snow onset date) is unknown.}
    \label{fig:hs_series_with_gap}
\end{figure}

The former group, instead, is characterized by HS measurements that, when converted into snow cover series, indicate the presence of snow on the ground 
for most of the hydrological years. This behaviour results from snow depth values that rarely reach zero. Such anomalies may be caused by a bias in the 
measuring instrument, which incorrectly identifies the ground level as being a few centimetres above its actual position, or by systematic errors in 
recording snow free conditions during summer. An example of each of these cases is shown in Figure \ref{fig:hs_series_wrong_summer}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{swe_calibration/hs_series_wrong_summer.png}
    \caption{(a) HS time series showing an incorrect identification of the ground level, with snow depth values remaining approximately constant at 
    $\sim \SI{10}{\centi\meter}$ throughout the summer. (b) HS time series showing anomalous summer spikes, clearly caused by erroneous readings. Snow 
    cover during the hottest months is indeed not possible at Rumo Lanza, which is located at $\SI{1100}{\meter}$ a.s.l.}
    \label{fig:hs_series_wrong_summer}
\end{figure}

Clearly, missing values and erroneous summer readings are not mutually exclusive, and more complex numerical anomalies may therefore occur within the same 
pseudo-hydrological year. Furthermore, several HS series were affected by other numerical artifacts, such as spikes or sudden drops in snow depth. Given these 
issues, specific procedures were developed to improve the quality of the snow depth dataset before proceeding with the calibration. The remainder of this 
section is thus devoted to describing separately the methods adopted to improve the accuracy of the station metadata and to correct, whenever possible 
and necessary, the numerical issues that initially compromised the reliability of snow depth data.



\subsection{Position correction}

The main issue affecting the unreliable coordinates was that most of them had been rounded to the nearest arc-minute. In the complex topography of the 
Alps, this spatial resolution, corresponding to roughly $\SI{2}{\kilo\meter}$, can result in elevation differences of several hundred meters, 
potentially affecting the representativeness of the reported station location. For this reason, the metadata of each station with unreliable 
coordinates were manually reviewed to identify its precise position, an important step for ensuring the accuracy of the subsequent model 
calibration. The review involved consulting the catalogues of the agencies responsible for the different stations. While this process did not lead 
to any improvement in some cases, such as for ARPA Lombardia, it allowed the retrieval of the exact coordinates of dozen of stations managed by 
WSL Institute for Snow and Avalanche Research SLF, Meteoswiss, Meteo France and the Austrian Hydrological Service. The updated coordinates were 
then inspected using Google Earth to visually verify whether the newly proposed sites corresponded to meteorological stations, as illustrated in panel 
(a) of Figure \ref{fig:station_search}. Panel (b), on the other hand, displays the original locations relative to their true positions, highlighting 
that most corrections involved shifts of less than $\SI{1}{\kilo\meter}$.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{swe_calibration/station_search.png}
    \caption{(a) Example of a Swiss AWS viewed from satellite imagery. The original coordinates placed the station approximately $\SI{1}{\kilo\meter}$ 
    east of its actual location. (b) Original station locations relative to their corrected positions, indicated by the red cross at the center of 
    the plot.}
    \label{fig:station_search}
\end{figure}

Overall, this procedure allowed the precise coordinates of approximately \num{300} stations to be recovered, increasing the number of stations whose 
elevations were consistent with the DEM from \num{532} to \num{829}. For the remaining ones, the original coordinates were retained because no 
sufficiently reliable sources were available to support a correction.



\subsection{HS series correction}

Before any corrections were applied to the snow depth time series, the data were visually inspected to identify the main issues and develop suitable 
procedures to address them. For each station, comparison plots were generated for all hydrological years containing at least one non-missing HS value, 
as illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:visual_check_example}. Each plot consisted of three panels: the first displayed the original HS time series, the 
second one showed the corresponding snow water equivalent (SWE) obtained using the $\Delta$Snow model, while the third presented the SWE simulated 
using the melt parameters optimized by \cite{Magnusson_DDF} and a temperature threshold of $\SI{2.5}{\degreeCelsius}$ for precipitation phase 
partitioning.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{swe_calibration/visual_check_example.png}
    \caption{Example of a comparison plot used to visually identify numerical artifacts in the HS series. The three panels show (a) the original 
    snow depth measurements, (b) the corresponding SWE values obtained using the $\Delta$Snow model, and (c) the simulated SWE. The latter was 
    obtained using the melt parameters optimized by \cite{Magnusson_DDF} and a temperature threshold of $\SI{2.5}{\degreeCelsius}$ for phase 
    partitioning. The red shaded areas indicate the summer window to be automatically set to zero.}
    \label{fig:visual_check_example}
\end{figure}
Although using model outputs to identify errors in the observations may seem circular, the relatively high temperature threshold adopted for 
precipitation phase partitioning makes this approach more reliable for distinguishing numerical issues from actual snow. Indeed, with such a threshold, 
the model tends to overestimate the number of snowfall events, as some rainfall may meet the requirements for solid precipitation computation. 
Consequently, when a spike in snow depth, such as the one observed in early September in Figure \ref{fig:visual_check_example}, is not accompanied by a 
corresponding increase in simulated SWE, it can reasonably be attributed to a numerical artifact rather than to actual snowfall. Each comparison plot 
also included a red shaded interval corresponding to a period typically associated with bare ground conditions. HS values within this window were proposed 
to be automatically set to zero. The duration of this interval varies depending on the elevation of the station as shown in Table \ref{tab:time_window_to_zero}:

During the preliminary visual inspection, hydrological years unsuitable for reconstruction were identified and flagged for exclusion. A 
pseudo-hydrological year was discarded when measurement noise could not be reliably distinguished from the actual snow signal or when the HS series 
contained too many missing values to extract meaningful information. This applied in particular to all series beginning after snow accumulation had already 
started, or ending before snow depth had returned to zero, as the onset or end of the snow season could not be determined accurately. As a result, 
most stations located in ski areas were deleted from the HS dataset, because their measurements typically did not cover the entire snow cycle. 
Moreover, the main numerical artifacts for each station were recorded, together with any necessary adjustments to the proposed summer zeroing window. 
Based on these observations, a set of correction steps was developed and subsequently applied to each station separately. Although this semi-automated 
approach required considerable manual effort, it was necessary to address the variety of numerical artifacts affecting individual stations. The denoising 
and gap-filling procedure comprised the following steps:
\begin{itemize}[label=$\blacktriangleright$]
    \item \textit{series filtering}, which consisted of discarding all pseudo-hydrological year previously flagged as unsuitable for signal 
    reconstruction. In addition, negative HS values were reclassified as missing data, since snow depth must always be positive or equal to zero.
    \item \textit{summer zeroing}, in which the selected summer window was automatically set to zero. For pseudo-hydrological years characterized 
    by a ground-level bias over the entire period, as in panel (a) of Figure \ref{fig:hs_series_wrong_summer}, the summer average HS value was first 
    computed and subtracted from every daily measurement, and only then was the proposed window zeroed. Any values that became negative because of 
    noise fluctuations were clipped to zero at this stage.
    \item \textit{spike and drop detection}, aimed at identifying such numerical artifacts and reclassifying them as missing data. Particular 
    attention was paid to spikes higher than $\SI{80}{\centi\meter}$ and drops larger than $\SI{50}{\centi\meter}$.
    \item \textit{gap filling}, which consisted of filling gaps bounded either by two strictly positive values or by two zeros with a linear interpolation 
    between the endpoints. Although this approach cannot recover the actual daily snow depth within the gaps, it allows snow covered ground to be 
    distinguished from bare ground. Restricting interpolation to gaps whose endpoints are both positive or both zero limits the cases in which snow depth 
    values are assigned to days when the presence of snow is uncertain.
    \item \textit{first visual inspection}, carried out to verify that the gaps had been filled correctly and to decide how to proceed. Whenever the 
    gap filling strategy produced faulty reconstructions, it was repeated selecting which gap to fill. 
    \item \textit{gap to zero}, which consisted of replacing all remaining missing values with zeros. Although this may seem somewhat crude, it does 
    not introduce significant artifacts, because series whose gaps substantially affected the onset or the end of the snow season had already been 
    discarded during series filtering. In the worst case, only a couple of days per pseudo-hydrological year were erroneously assigned to zero.
    \item \textit{zero under threshold}, applied only to series in which the first visual inspection had revealed minor residual noise. In such cases, all 
    HS values below a given threshold, typically a few centimetres, were set to zero.
    \item \textit{final visual inspection}, performed to assess the plausibility of the corrected series. Any remaining issues were addressed on a case 
    by case basis.
\end{itemize}
Figure \ref{fig:hs_correction_steps} illustrates how a typical HS time series changed throughout the main stages of the correction procedure.

\end{figure}


\subsection{Final dataset}

The snow depth denoising and gap filling procedure described in the previous subsection led to the removal of \num{220} stations, reducing the network from 
\num{1052} to \num{832}. The total number of pseudo-hydrological years also decreased from \num{27388} to \num{20248}, corresponding to a reduction 
of approximately \num{26}\%. Despite these changes, the spatial coverage and distribution of the network were largely preserved, as the remaining station 
positions were adjusted by no more than $\SI{1.5}{\kilo\meter}$. The elevation distribution was also similar, with both the original and the corrected 
networks mainly concentrated between \num{1000} and \SI{2000}{\meter} above sea level. In contrast, the consistency between the reported station elevation 
and the ALOS AW3D30 DEM improved considerably, as illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:station_compatibility_before_after_QC}. In fact, after the corrections, 
\num{714} out of the \num{832} remaining stations passed the elevation reliability check and are therefore shown in green in panel (b).

\section{Calibration procedure}

The correction of station metadata proved particularly relevant at this stage, as the calibration procedure required accurate coordinates. 
Consequently, of the \num{832} stations in the final dataset, only the \num{714} considered reliable were retained for the subsequent analysis. The 
first step of model calibration was the reconstruction of daily temperature and precipitation series at the exact location of each station over the 
1951--2023 period. This operation was successful for \num{632} stations, while for the remaining \num{82} the available data were insufficient to 
obtain accurate results. These reconstructed meteorological data could then be used to simulate snow water equivalent series for comparison with 
the corresponding snow depth observations. Although both variables reflect the amount of snow on the ground, they represent different physical 
quantities and cannot be compared directly. A possible solution to this problem would be to use a snow model such as $\Delta$Snow, which converts 
HS series into SWE records by interpreting snow depth increases and decreases exceeding a certain threshold $\tau$ as snowfall and snowmelt events 
respectively. However, integrating $\Delta$Snow into the calibration procedure would also introduce additional uncertainties, related to the 
assumptions and structural limitations of the model itself. To avoid this issue, the calibration was based on snow presence rather than on the 
absolute values of HS and SWE. Both time series were therefore converted into binary sequences, assigning a value of \num{1} to days with positive 
snow depth or snow water equivalent, while zeros were left unchanged. The aim of the calibration was then to maximize the temporal overlap between 
simulated and observed snow covered days. To quantify this agreement, the following loss function was adopted:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}\,=\,1\,-\,\text{CSI}, 
    \label{eq:loss_function}
\end{equation}
where CSI stands for Critical Success Index, defined as 
\begin{equation}
    \text{CSI}\,=\,\frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP}\,+\,\text{FP}\,+\,\text{FN}}.
    \label{eq:csi}
\end{equation}
In Equation \eqref{eq:csi} TP, FP and FN denote true positive, false positive and false negatives respectively. This means that the adopted loss 
function focuses on snow, without considering true negatives, i.e. days in which both series indicate bare ground conditions.



\subsection{Homogeneous calibration}

The first calibration attempt focused exclusively on the temperature threshold for precipitation phase partitioning $T_\tau$, while the snowmelt 
parameters were fixed at the values proposed by \cite{Magnusson_DDF}, reported in Table \ref{tab:optimal_parameters}.

loss function is shown in panel (a) of Figure \ref{fig:homogenoeus_calib}. A single, well-defined minimum occurs near \SI{1}{\degreeCelsius}, with the

to overestimate snowpack persistence, as illustrated in panel (b) of the same picture.

A first insight into the model behaviour can be gained by examining the monthly agreement between simulated SWE and observed HS series, as shown in Figure 
\ref{fig:homogeneous_calib_monthly_wise}. In particular, during the first part of the snow season, the model markedly overestimates the presence of snow 
on the ground compared to the HS series. This mismatch may be partially attributed to the fact that the model does not account for the individual heat 
fluxes contributing to the energy balance of the snowpack. Specifically, during the early winter months, the ground releases the heat stored over the 
summer as it approaches thermal equilibrium limiting the persistence of snow by favouring the melting of newly formed snow layers. Consequently, the 
observed snow depth drops to zero sooner than the simulated one after an ephemeral snowfall, since this particular source of heat is not considered in 
the modelling chain. In addition, the model appears to predict complete snowmelt too early, suggesting that the DDF parameters proposed by \cite{Magnusson_DDF} 
may be excessively high for the study area. This second inaccuracy in representing the duration of snow season may also partially explain the overestimation 
observed at the beginning of the hydrological year. Indeed, minimizing the loss function may favour a certain number of false positives early in the season 
if this leads to more true positives later on.

A second insight could be attained by focusing on the elevation of the snow depth stations used in the calibration procedure. To this end, the 
HS series were divided into three elevation bands, each containing approximately the same number of stations, and the confusion matrices shown in Figure 
\ref{fig:homogeneous_calib_elevation_wise} were produced. This analysis highlighted that the model overestimates snow cover persistence at low and 
intermediate elevation, whereas this tendency reverses above \SI{2000}{\meter} a.s.l. This behaviour may be partially attributable to the model inputs.
Indeed, at high elevations solid precipitation is typically underestimated because of snow undercatch by unheated rain gauges.

Although \cite{precipitation_IHGAR} applied quality checks to exclude clearly biased precipitation series from the generation of the precipitation rasters, 
some issues may persist. Moreover, below \SI{1000}{\meter} a.s.l. the ground rarely freezes, meaning that ground heat flux $G$ could be responsible for 
the larger mismatch between observed HS and simulated SWE series.



\subsection{Simulated annealing}

The results of the homogeneous calibration highlighted some limitations in the model ability to accurately reproduce the evolution 
of seasonal snow cover. In particular, a widespread underestimation of snow cover persistence during spring months suggested that 
the snowmelt parameters proposed by \cite{Magnusson_DDF} might not be optimal for the study area and could be partially responsible 
for the observed discrepancies. Consequently, a second calibration was performed, in which the optimization was extended to 
the complete set $\left(T_{\tau},\,T_{melt},\,\text{DDF}_{ave},\,\text{DDF}_{amp},\,m_m \right)$. To explore this multidimensional 
parameter space, a simulated annealing approach was adopted. This stochastic optimization technique is inspired by the physical 
annealing itself, in which a material is heated and subsequently cooled at a sufficiently slow rate to allow its internal structure 
to approach the ground state, i.e. the lowest possible energy \parencite{SA_1}. The fundamental assumption of this algorithm is to 
interpret the cost function $\mathcal{L}\left(x\right)$ as the energy of a fictitious statistical-mechanics system, whose Boltzmann 
distribution is given by
\begin{equation}
    p\left(x\right)\,=\,\exp[-\beta \mathcal{L} \left(x\right)]/Z.
    \label{eq:boltzmann_dis_SA}
\end{equation}
In Equation \eqref{eq:boltzmann_dis_SA} $Z$ denotes the partition function and $\beta\,=\,1/T$ is the inverse temperature, which has 
no physical meaning in this context, but acts as a control parameter. The optimization procedure consist of generating configurations 
in the parameter space based on their actual probability, given by Equation \eqref{eq:boltzmann_dis_SA}, while also allowing for a 
gentle decrease in temperature T from high values toward zero. Sampling from the distribution at a given temperature is performed 
using the Metropolis algorithm \parencite{Metropolis}. Starting from a configuration $x$, a candidate one $x'$ is generated in 
the neighborhood $N$ of $x$ by randomly perturbating the parameters and is accepted with probability 
\begin{equation}
    P_{\mathrm{acc}} = \min\left\{1,\; \exp\left(-\frac{\Delta_{x',x}}{T}\right)\right\},
    \qquad \Delta_{x',x} = \mathcal{L}(x') - \mathcal{L}(x).
    \label{eq:metropolis_criterion}
\end{equation}
The form of Equation \eqref{eq:metropolis_criterion} implies that moves reducing the loss function are always accepted, whereas 
configurations associated with a loss increase are selected with a probability depending on both the magnitude of the increase and 
the simulated temperature. The behaviour of the algorithm is ultimately governed by the cooling schedule, which specifies the 
temperature sequence $\{T_k\}$ together with the number of $\{M_k\}$ of Metropolis moves performed at each temperature level. 
The initial temperature $T_0$ should be sufficiently larger than the typical loss variations, allowing for an early stage of 
random search within the admissible parameter space $\Omega$. Indeed, at the beginning of the simulated annealing, approximately 
all proposed moves are accepted, sometimes resulting in a temporary increase in loss function value. Although this behaviour may 
appear counterproductive at first, it constitutes one of the key strengths of simulated annealing, as it allows the algorithm to 
excape local minima, explore alternative region of the space parameter, and potentially converge towards a solution with a lower 
loss function value. Furthermore, as the system cools, the Boltzmann distribution becomes increasingly concentrated around the 
global minima of $\mathcal{L} \left(x\right)$, thus allowing for the identification of the optimal parameter values. Within this 
framework, the choice of the cooling rate is critical, as it involves significant tradeoffs between solution quality and computational 
cost: an abrupt transition from high to low temperatures may cause the stochastic search to become trapped in a suboptimal local 
minimum, whereas an excessively slow schedule requires a prohibitive number of loss evaluations, resulting in impractical 
computational times. The typical structure of the Simulated Annealing (SA) algorithm is therefore:

Select an initial solution $x\,\in\,\Omega$
Select an initial temperature $T\,=\,T_0$
Select a cooling scheme $\left\{\left(T_0,\,M_0\right),\,\left(T_1,\,M_1\right),\,\dots\right\}$
Select temperature index k

(*@\textit{Repeat}@*)
Set repetition counter $m\,=\,0$
    (*@\textit{Repeat}@*)
    Generate a solution $x'\,\in\,N\left(x\right)$
    Calculate $\Delta_{x',x}\,=\,\mathcal{L} \left(x'\right)\,-\,\mathcal{L} \left(x\right)$
    If $\Delta_{x',x} \leq 0$, then $x\,\leftarrow\,x'$
    If $\Delta_{x',x} > 0$, then $x\,\leftarrow\,x'$ with probability $\exp\left(-\Delta_{x',x}/T_k\right)$
    $m \leftarrow m + 1$
    Until $m\,=\,M_k$
$k \leftarrow k + 1$
