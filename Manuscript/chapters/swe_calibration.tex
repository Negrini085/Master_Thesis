\section{HS data}

The snow depth dataset used to calibrate the degree-day model described in the previous chapter was created through a data rescue activity. It combines 
HS time series from \cite{Matiu_HS} with data provided by national and regional meteorological and hydrological services, including ARPA Piemonte, Centro 
Funzionale della Valle d'Aosta, Meteoswiss, WSL Institute for Snow and Avalanche Research SLF, ARPA Lombardia, Meteotrentino, ARPA Veneto, and the Austrian 
Hydrological Service \parencite{hs_data}. The final dataset includes 1052 stations, mostly automatic, distributed across the entire Alpine Arc, as shown 
in Figure \ref{fig:hs_series_origin}. Depending on the station, these time series can extend back to the 1960s, although most observations date from 1990 
onwards.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{swe_calibration/hs_series_origin.jpg}
    \caption{Spatial distribution of the stations that made up the original dataset. Each station is marked with the symbol associated with the 
    data source from which the data were obtained. Image from \cite{hs_data}.}
    \label{fig:hs_series_origin}
\end{figure}

The original HS dataset, however, presented several issues that needed to be addressed before it could be considered suitable for further analysis. The 
first reliability check that was done focused on the accuracy of the station metadata, specifically longitude, latitude and elevation. To this end, the 
ALOS AW3D30 digital elevation model (DEM) was used to compare the reported elevation of each station with the elevation of the DEM pixel nearest to its 
coordinates. A compatibility threshold of $\SI{12}{\meter}$ was adopted. Although this criterion is relatively strict given the complex topography of the 
Alpine Arc, it was considered appropriate due to the high spatial resolution of the DEM, namely $\SI{30}{\meter}$. Based on this threshold, more than 
half of the stations were classified as unreliable, as show in Figure \ref{fig:station_distribution_original}.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{swe_calibration/station_distribution_original.png}
    \caption{(a) Spatial distribution of stations with reliable (unreliable) metadata. (b) Frequency histogram of the differences between the reported 
    station elevations and those derived from the ALOS AW3D30 DEM. In both panels, green represents reliable stations, for which the absolute elevation 
    difference didn't exceed $\SI{12}{\meter}$, while red indicates stations that didn't meet this criterion.}
    \label{fig:station_distribution_original}
\end{figure}
Moreover, a second quality check revealed that not only the station metadata, but also the snow depth time series required further processing. In fact, to 
investigate potential issues within the snow depth dataset, HS series were compared with MODIS observations for stations falling within the study area 
and the period covered by \cite{MODIS_ITA}. For each station in this subset, the MODIS time series was extracted from the nearest grid point, while the HS 
data were converted into binary snow cover series using a threshold of $\SI{0}{\centi\meter}$: positive snow depth values were assigned a value of one, 
whereas zeros remained unchanged. Both time series were then organized in pseudo-hydrological years, extending from 1 September of a given year to 31 
August of the following one, to allow for a direct comparison. A perfect match was not expected, as station measurements are collected in-situ, whereas 
MODIS provides spatially distributed observations with pixels classified as snow free if the snow cover fraction does not exceed \num{0.5}. Nevertheless, 
the comparison shown in Figure \ref{fig:comparison_HS_MODIS_original} revealed substantial discrepancies between the two datasets.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{swe_calibration/comparison_HS_MODIS_original.png}
    \caption{(a) Comparison between SCD values computed from HS series and MODIS data. Each dot represents a pseudo-hydrological year, which is the 
    period spanning from 1 September of a given year to 31 August of the following one. Blue dots are data retrieved from reliable stations, whereas 
    orange ones are not. (b) Confusion matrix comparing snow cover observations according to MODIS and HS data.}
    \label{fig:comparison_HS_MODIS_original}
\end{figure}
In particular, the comparison between MODIS and HS time series showed a considerable scatter in SCD values, as visible in panel (a) of Figure 
\ref{fig:comparison_HS_MODIS_original}. Two distinct groups of data point deviating markedly from the identity line can indeed be identified: the first 
is concentrated in the upper-left corner of the plot, while the second one lies much closer to the x-axis than expected. The latter group mainly consists 
of hydrological years characterized by numerous and/or prolonged gaps in the snow depth records, with observations missing for much of the snow season. 
A representative example is shown in Figure \ref{fig:hs_series_with_gap}.  

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{swe_calibration/hs_series_with_gap.png}
    \caption{Example of an HS time series affected by extensive data gaps (highlighted in green). This series cannot be reliably reconstructed using 
    standard gap-filling methods because the SOD (snow onset date) is unknown.}
    \label{fig:hs_series_with_gap}
\end{figure}

The former group, instead, is characterized by HS measurements that, when converted into snow cover series, indicate the presence of snow on the ground 
for most of the hydrological years. This behaviour results from snow depth values that rarely reach zero. Such anomalies may be caused by a bias in the 
measuring instrument, which incorrectly identifies the ground level as being a few centimetres above its actual position, or by systematic errors in 
recording snow free conditions during summer. An example of each of these cases is shown in Figure \ref{fig:hs_series_wrong_summer}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{swe_calibration/hs_series_wrong_summer.png}
    \caption{(a) HS time series showing an incorrect identification of the ground level, with snow depth values remaining approximately constant at 
    $\sim \SI{10}{\centi\meter}$ throughout the summer. (b) HS time series showing anomalous summer spikes, clearly caused by erroneous readings. Snow 
    cover during the hottest months is indeed not possible at Rumo Lanza, which is located at $\SI{1100}{\meter}$ a.s.l.}
    \label{fig:hs_series_wrong_summer}
\end{figure}

Clearly, missing values and erroneous summer readings are not mutually exclusive, and more complex numerical anomalies may therefore occur within the same 
pseudo-hydrological year. Moreover, several HS series are affected by other numerical artifacts, such as spikes or sudden drops in snow depth. Given these 
issues, specific procedures were developed to improve the quality of the snow depth dataset before proceeding with the calibration. The remainder of this 
section is devoted to describing separately the methods adopted to improve the accuracy of the station metadata and to correct, whenever possible and 
necessary, the numerical artifacts that initially compromised the reliability of the data.



\subsection{Position correction}

The main issue with the unreliable coordinates was that most of them had been rounded to the nearest arc-minute. In the complex topography of the 
Alps, this spatial resolution, corresponding to roughly $\SI{2}{\kilo\meter}$, can result in elevation differences of several hundred meters, 
potentially affecting the representativeness of the reported station location. For this reason, the metadata of each station with unreliable 
coordinates were manually reviewed to identify its precise position, an important step for ensuring the accuracy of the subsequent model 
calibration. The review involved consulting the catalogues of the agencies responsible for the different stations. While this process did not lead 
to any improvement in some cases, such as for ARPA Lombardia, it allowed the retrieval of the exact coordinates of dozen of stations managed by 
WSL Institute for Snow and Avalanche Research SLF, Meteoswiss, Meteo France and the Austrian Hydrological Service. The updated coordinates were 
then inspected using Google Earth to visually verify whether the identified sites corresponded to meteorological stations, as illustrated in panel 
(a) of Figure \ref{fig:station_search}. Panel (b), on the other hand, displays the original locations relative to their true positions, highlighting 
that most corrections involved shifts of less than $\SI{1}{\kilo\meter}$.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{swe_calibration/station_search.png}
    \caption{(a) Example of a Swiss AWS viewed from satellite imagery. The original coordinates placed the station approximately $\SI{1}{\kilo\meter}$ 
    east of its actual location. (b) Original station locations relative to their corrected positions, indicated by the red cross at the center of 
    the plot.}
    \label{fig:station_search}
\end{figure}

Overall, this procedure allowed the precise coordinates of approximately \num{300} stations to be recovered, increasing the number of stations whose 
elevations were consistent with the DEM from \num{532} to \num{829}. For the remaining stations, the original coordinates were retained because no 
sufficiently reliable sources were available to support a correction.



\subsection{HS series correction}

Before applying any corrections to the snow depth time series, the data were visually inspected to identify the main issues and develop suitable 
correction procedures. For each station, comparison plots were generated for all hydrological years containing at least one non-missing HS value, 
as illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:visual_check_example}. Each plot consisted of three panels: the first displayed the original HS time series, the 
second one showed the corresponding snow water equivalent (SWE) obtained using the $\Delta$Snow model, while the third presented the SWE simulated 
using the melt parameters optimized by \cite{Magnusson_DDF} and a temperature threshold of $\SI{2.5}{\degreeCelsius}$ for precipitation phase 
partitioning.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{swe_calibration/visual_check_example.png}
    \caption{Example of a comparison plot used to visually identify numerical artifacts in the HS series. The three panels show (a) the original 
    snow depth measurements, (b) the corresponding SWE values obtained using the $\Delta$Snow model, and (c) the simulated SWE. The latter was 
    obtained using the melt parameters optimized by \cite{Magnusson_DDF} and a temperature threshold of $\SI{2.5}{\degreeCelsius}$ for phase 
    partitioning. The red shaded areas indicate the summer window to be automatically set to zero.}
    \label{fig:visual_check_example}
\end{figure}
Although using model outputs to identify errors in the observations may appear circular, the relatively high temperature threshold adopted for 
precipitation phase partitioning provides additional confidence in distinguishing numerical issues from actual snowfall events. In particular, 
when a spike in snow depth, such as the one observed in early September in Figure \ref{fig:visual_check_example}, is not accompanied by a 
corresponding increase in the simulated SWE, it can reasonably be considered a numerical artifact since with a high temperature threshold the number 
of snowfall events is overestimated, since even rainfall sometimes meets the requirements for solid precipitation computation. Each comparison plot 
also included a red shaded interval corresponding to a period typically associated with bare ground. HS values within this window were proposed to 
be automatically set to zero. The duration of this interval varies depending on the elevation of the station as shown in Table \ref{tab:time_window_to_zero}:

During the preliminary visual inspection, hydrological years unsuitable for reconstruction were identified and flagged for exclusion. This was 
necessary when missing values were too numerous to extract meaningful information from the HS series, or when measurement noise could not reliably 
be distinguished from the actual snow signal. In particular, all series ending before the snow depth had returned to zero, or beginning after snow 
accumulation had already started, were discarded. In these cases the onset or end of the snow season could not be determined accurately, making a 
proper reconstruction impossible. This truncation of the HS series led to the exclusion of the majority of stations located in ski areas, where 
measurements typically did not cover the entire snow cycle. Moreover, the main numerical artifacts for each station were recorded, together with 
any necessary adjustments to the proposed summer zeroing window. Based on these observations, a set of correction steps was developed and 
subsequently applied to each station separately. Although this semi-automated approach required substantial manual effort, it was necessary to 
account for the different types of numerical artifacts affecting individual stations. The denoising and gap-filling procedure consisted of the 
following steps:
\begin{itemize}[label=$\blacktriangleright$]
    \item \textit{series filtering}, which consisted of discarding all pseudo-hydrological year previously flagged as unsuitable for signal 
    reconstruction. In addition, negative HS values were reclassified as missing data, since snow depth must always be bigger or equal to zero.
    \item \textit{summer zeroing}, in which the selected summer window was automatically set to zero. For pseudo-hydrological years characterized 
    by a ground-level bias over the entire period, as in panel (a) of Figure \ref{fig:hs_series_wrong_summer}, the average HS value was first 
    computed and subtracted from every daily measurement, and only then was the summer window zeroed. Any values that became negative because of 
    noise fluctuations were clipped to zero at this stage.
    \item \textit{spike and drop detection}, aimed at identifying such numerical artifacts and reclassifying them as missing data. ?articular 
    attention was paid to spiked higher than $\SI{80}{\centi\meter}$ and to drops larger than $\SI{50}{\centi\meter}$.
    \item \textit{gap filling}, which consisted of linearly interpolating between the endpoints of every gap bounded by two strictly positive values 
    or by two zeros.  This approach clearly cannot reconstruct the actual snow depth on each day within the gap, but nevertheless makes it possible 
    to distinguish between bare and snow covered ground. Requiring both endpoints to be positive or zero limits the cases in which the interpolation 
    assigns positive snow depth values to days when the presence of snow is uncertain.
    \item \textit{first visual inspection}, carried out to verify that the gaps had been filled correctly and to decide how to proceed. Whenever the 
    gap filling strategy produced faulty reconstructions, it was repeated selecting which gap to fill. 
    \item \textit{gap to zero}, which consisted of replacing all remaining missing values with zeros. Although this may seem somewhat crude, it does 
    not introduce significant artifacts, because series whose gaps substantially affected the start or the end of the snow season had already been 
    discarded during series filtering. In the worst case, only a couple of days per pseudo-hydrological year are forced to zero. 
    \item \textit{zero under threshold}, applied only when the first visual inspection had revealed minor residual noise. In such cases, all days 
    with HS below a given threshold, typically a couple of centimetres, were set to zero.
    \item \textit{final visual inspection}, performed to assess the plausibility of the corrected series. Any remaining issues were addressed case 
    by case.
\end{itemize}
Figure \ref{fig:hs_correction_steps} illustrates how a typical HS time series changed throughout the main stages of the correction procedure.

\subsection{Final dataset}

The two procedures developed to improve the dataset quality resulted in the removal of \num{220} stations, reducing the total number from 1052 to 832. 
Furthermore, the total number of hydrological years decreased from \num{27388} to \num{20248}, representing a \num{26}\% reduction. The spatial 
distribution of snow depth stations didn't change substantially, as they were moved at most by $\SI{1.5}{\kilo\meter}$. A sensible change can be instead 
detected on the number of station whose metadata are deemed as reliable by comparing them with the ALOS AW3D30 DEM, as shown in Figure 
\ref{fig:station_compatibility_before_after_QC}. In fact, on the total of 832 final stations, as many as 714 passed the consistency check, and are 
consequently colored green in panel (b) of the same figure. Regarding the elevation distribution, no major changes are detected. In fact, both original 
and corrected networks are more focused on the medium elevations, with the most represented elevation band being the one comprised between \num{1000} and 
\SI{2000}{\meter} a.s.l.

\section{Calibration procedure}

The previously performed correction of the station metadata is particularly relevant at this stage, as accurate knowledge of the stations' geographical 
coordinates is essential to ensure proper calibration. From now on, only the 714 reliable stations of the 832 constituing the corrected dataset were 
taken into account. For each of their coordinates, a reconstruction of daily temperature and precipitation fields over the 1951--2023 period was attempted. 
This procedure was successful for only 632 station out of the 714, since in some cases there were too few data to obtain reliable outputs. Having this 
temperature and precipitation data made it possible to reconstruct SWE series for each of the remaining stations, which could then be compared with the 
corresponding snow depth series, since both quantify the amount of snow within the snowpack, but through different unit of measurements. However, the 
conversion from one to another is not straightforward, since it requires the knowledge of the vertical profile of snow density, which in the framework of 
this thesis remains unknown. This issue can be solved by means of a snow model such as $\Delta$Snow, which makes the conversion from snow depth to 
snow water equivalent under the assumption that every increase of more than a threshold $\tau$ in snow depth can be interpreted as a snowfall event. This 
multi layered simulation of the snowpack even predicts snow compaction by mean of a Newtonian approach. Within the framework of this thesis, however, 
it was decided to not make a direct comparison between model outputs and observational data, given that the usage of $\Delta$Snow during the calibration 
would have made it susceptible to modelling errors and uncertainties within $\Delta$Snow itself. The chosen approach consisted instead in converting both 
HS and SWE series into binary ones, labelling as zero the days with \SI{0}{\centi\meter} of snow depth (or \SI{0}{\milli\meter} of SWE), whereas positive 
values got labelled as ones. The main focus of the calibration was then the temporal allignement of these binary series, with the goal of making them 
overlap in snow presence prediction as much as possible. The loss function chosen to compare model outputs with snow depth time series is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}\,=\,1\,-\,\text{CSI}, 
    \label{eq:loss_function}
\end{equation}
where CSI stands for the Critical Success Index, computed as 
\begin{equation}
    \text{CSI}\,=\,\frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP}\,+\,\text{FP}\,+\,\text{FN}}.
    \label{eq:csi}
\end{equation}
This means that the loss function effectively focuses on the snow event, disregarding true negative predictions (i.e. zero to zero correspondance in 
binary series).
