\section{HS data}

The snow depth dataset used to calibrate the degree-day model described in the previous chapter was created through a data rescue activity. It combines 
HS time series from \cite{Matiu_HS} with data provided by national and regional meteorological and hydrological services, including ARPA Piemonte, Centro 
Funzionale della Valle d'Aosta, Meteoswiss, WSL Institute for Snow and Avalanche Research SLF, ARPA Lombardia, Meteotrentino, ARPA Veneto, and the Austrian 
Hydrological Service \parencite{hs_data}. The final dataset includes 1052 stations, mostly automatic, distributed across the entire Alpine Arc, as shown 
in Figure \ref{fig:hs_series_origin}. Depending on the station, these time series can extend back to the 1960s, although most observations date from 1990 
onwards.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{swe_calibration/hs_series_origin.jpg}
    \caption{Spatial distribution of the stations that made up the original dataset. Each station is marked with the symbol associated with the 
    data source from which the data were obtained. Image from \cite{hs_data}.}
    \label{fig:hs_series_origin}
\end{figure}

The original HS dataset, however, was affected by several issues that needed to be addressed before it could be considered suitable for 
further analysis. The first reliability check that was done focused on assessing the accuracy of the station metadata, namely longitude, latitude 
and elevation. In particular, the ALOS AW3D30 digital elevation model was used to compare the reported elevation of each station with that of the DEM 
pixel closest to its coordinates. A compatibility threshold of $\SI{12}{\meter}$ was adopted: although this criterion is rather stringent given the 
complex topography of the Alpine Arc, it was considered appropriate given the high spatial resolution of the DEM, namely $\SI{30}{\meter}$. With this 
threshold, more than half of the stations were classified as unreliable, as show in Figure \ref{fig:station_distribution_original}.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{swe_calibration/station_distribution_original.png}
    \caption{(a) Spatial distribution of stations with reliable (unreliable) metadata. (b) Frequency histogram of the elevation differences between the 
    reported station elevations and those derived from the ALOS AW3D30 DEM. In both panels, green indicates reliable stations, i.e. stations for which the 
    absolute difference between the reported and DEM-derived elevations does not exceed $\SI{12}{\meter}$, while red is used for stations that did 
    not satisfy this criterion.}
    \label{fig:station_distribution_original}
\end{figure}
Moreover, a second quality check showed that not only the station metadata, but also the snow depth time series required direct processing. In fact, 
a comparison between HS series and MODIS observations was made for those stations enclosed on both the study area and the covered period of \cite{MODIS_ITA} 
in order to attain information on eventual patologies within the dataset. MODIS series were extracted for each grid point that was the closest to a given 
station of the investigated subset, while HS series were converted in snow cover ones using $\SI{0}{\centi\meter}$ as depth threshold: above that, the 
snow depth values were substituted with one, otherwise with zero. Both time series for each station were then organized in pseudo-hydrological years, 
spanning from 1 September of a given year to 31 August of the following one, allowing for a direct comparison. Although a perfect match was not the 
expected outcome, since station data are recorded in-situ, whereas MODIS makes spatialized observations, with pixels being classified as snow free 
when the SCF does not exceed \num{0.5}, however the comparison shown in Figure \ref{fig:comparison_HS_MODIS_original} is characterized by substantial 
disagreement. 
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{swe_calibration/comparison_HS_MODIS_original.png}
    \caption{(a) Comparison between SCD values computed from HS series and MODIS data. Each dot represents a pseudo-hydrological year, which is the 
    period spanning from 1 September of a given year to 31 August of the following one. Blue dots are data retrieved from stations which are reliable 
    as in Figure \ref{fig:station_distribution_original}, whereas orange ones are not. (b) Confusion matrix comparing snow presence according to 
    MODIS and HS data.}
    \label{fig:comparison_HS_MODIS_original}
\end{figure}
In particular, what emerges is a substantial overestimation of snow presence on the ground by station measurements: even though a 
slight bias toward HS measurements was expected, such a strong imbalance suggests that some numerical issues could be affecting HS data. Furthermore, 
in panel (a) of Figure \ref{fig:comparison_HS_MODIS_original} can be identified two distinct types of data points, both deviating markedly from the 
identity line, since the first group is concentrated in the upper-left corner of the plot, whereas the second one is located much closer to the x-axis 
than it should be. The latter family of points is mainly made up of hydrological years with several and/or big reading gaps, meaning that for the 
majority of the snow season snow depth is not recorded as it should be. A visual example of such a recording is shown in Figure \ref{fig:hs_series_with_gap}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{swe_calibration/hs_series_with_gap.png}
    \caption{Example of an HS series strongly influenced by missing values (i.e. the green parts). Such a series is not retriviable with typical 
    techniques, since the SOD is unknown.}
    \label{fig:hs_series_with_gap}
\end{figure}

The former one is instead characterized by HS readings which, converted in SC series, predict snow presence on the ground over most (if not all) the 
hydrological year. This behaviour is a consequence of a snow depth series that rarely reaches zero. The way in which this occur may be linked to a 
reading bias of the measuring instrument, which identifies the ground level as standing some centimeters above the ground, or to systematic errors 
while recording bare ground during summer. A couple of more examples are shown in Figure \ref{fig:hs_series_wrong_summer}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{swe_calibration/hs_series_wrong_summer.png}
    \caption{(a) HS series which exhibits a ground level misidentification, with summer values constant at $\sim \SI{10}{\centi\meter}$. (b) HS 
    series which exhibits summer spikes and misreading, given that Rumo Lanza stands at $\SI{1100}{\meter}$ a.s.l. and clearly can't experience 
    snow cover during the hottest months.}
    \label{fig:hs_series_wrong_summer}
\end{figure}

Clearly, missing values and summer faulty data are not mutually exclusive, so that even more complex and peculiar behaviour can be detected during 
a single pseudo-hydrological year. Moreover, other numerical artifacts such as spikes or sudden drops in snow depth affect several HS series. Given 
the presence of all these issues, it was necessary to develop specific techniques to improve the quality of the snow depth dataset before proceeding 
with the calibration. In the remainder of this section, the procedures adopted to improve the accuracy of the station metadata and to correct, 
whenever possible and necessary, the numerical artifacts that originally were making these data unusable will be presented separately.



\subsection{Position correction}

The main issue with the unreliable coordinates was that the vast majority of them had been approximated to the nearest arc-minute. In the complex 
topography of the Alps, this level of spatial uncertainty, corresponding to roughly $\SI{2}{\kilo\meter}$, may translate into elevation differences 
of several hundred meters and thus substantially affect the representativeness of the station location. For this reason, the metadata of each 
unreliable station were manually checked to retrieve its exact position, a crucial step for ensuring the accuracy of the subsequent model 
calibration. In particular, the metadata rescue procedure consisted in searching them in the catalogues of the agencies managing the various stations. 
While in some instances, such as with ARPA Lombardia, this review let to no improvement, in others, like WSL Institute for Snow and Avalanche Research 
SLF, it made it possible to recover the exact coordinates of dozen of stations. The new coordinates were then checked using Google Earth, in order 
to visually check if they actually corresponded to a meteorological station, as shown in panel (a) of Figure \ref{fig:station_search}. Panel (b), on 
the other hand, displays the difference between the original and actual position of the reviewed stations, showing that the vast majority of them 
was corrected for less than a kilometer.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{swe_calibration/station_search.png}
    \caption{(a) Example of a Swiss AWS viewed from satellite images. The original location was roughly $\SI{1}{\kilo\meter}$ east of the actual site. 
    (b) Comparison between original location and actual position of the reviewed stations. The actual position is the red cross on the center of the 
    plot.}
    \label{fig:station_search}
\end{figure}

The procedure presented above enabled the retrieval of roughly \num{300} locations, increasing the number of stations consistent with the DEM 
elevation from \num{532} to \num{829}. For the remainder of the station dataset, it was decided not to alter the unreliable positions due to the 
lack of reliable sources.



\subsection{HS series correction}

Before directly modifying the snow depth time series, they were visually inspected in order to develop procedures that could effectively address 
the issues present in the dataset. In particular, for each station, all hydrological years containing at least one numerical value (i.e. not 
consisting entirely of missing dataset) were considered to generate comparison plots such as the one shown in Figure \ref{fig:visual_check_example}. 
Each plot consisted of three rows: the first displayed the snow depth time series, the second one showed its conversion into SWE obtained using the 
Delta Snow model, while the third consisted of the model outputs obtained using the melt parameters optimized by \cite{Magnusson_DDF} and a 
temperature threshold of $\SI{2.5}{\degreeCelsius}$ for precipitation phase partitioning.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{swe_calibration/visual_check_example.png}
    \caption{Plot used to visually inspect the numerical issues of the HS series. A comparison between (a) snow depth values, (b) swe conversion of 
    HS reading and (c) model outputs is made. The model parameters used to produce the last series are: the melt parameters optimized by \cite{Magnusson_DDF} 
    and a temperature threshold of $\SI{2.5}{\degreeCelsius}$. The red part of each graph is the summer window to be automatically set to zero.}
    \label{fig:visual_check_example}
\end{figure}
Although this approach may seem circular, the relatively high threshold used to distinguish between snow and rain ensures that, if a spike in snow 
depth (such as the one observed in early September in Figure \ref{fig:visual_check_example}) is not accompanied by a corresponding one in the model 
outputs, it can be confidently attributed to a numerical artifact. In each of these comparison plots, a portion of the time series was coloured red. 
This highlighted time window, which covers to a period typically associated with bare ground conditions, is proposed to be automatically set to 
zero: its width varies with station declared elevation as reported in Table \ref{tab:time_window_to_zero}.

During the visual inspection of time series, for each station were signalled the hydrological years which could be deleted, both because missing data 
were so numerous that it was impossible to obtain useful information from the snow depth series, and because the noise could not be clearly 
decoupled from the snow reading. In particular, all HS series truncated before reaching zero or started after the beginning of accumulation were 
discarded, as the correct evaluation of the end or the beginning of the snow season respectively (and thus the reconstruction of the time series) 
was not possible. Due to this signal cut-offs, some stations operating within ski areas were discarded right away since recordings covered only the 
ski season, disregarding the rest of snow phenomena. Moreover, the main numerical artifacts for each station were reported, in addition to specifying 
whether the proposed window for automatic zeroing needed to be modified or not. Once all snow depth series had been visually inspected, the following 
procedures were developed in order to be later applied to one station at a time. This semi-automated procedure, which required a lot of human work, 
was necessary in order to ensure that the complex picture of numerical artifacts, typical of each station, was taken care of with attention. In 
particular, for each snow depth time series, the following steps were followed:

\begin{itemize}[label=$\blacktriangleright$]
    \item \textit{series filtering}, which consisted in discarding every pseudo-hydrological year that was previously labelled as not adequate for signal 
    reconstruction. Moreover, negative HS values were reclassified as missing data, since snow depth must always be non-negative.
    \item \textit{summer handling}, which consisted in setting automatically to zero the proposed time window. If a given pseudo-hydrological year had an 
    HS series like the one in panel (a) of Figure \ref{fig:hs_series_wrong_summer}, namely with a ground level bias over the entire investigated period, 
    before setting to zero the summer window, the average snow depth value was computed in order to subtract it to every daily HS measurement. If some values 
    became negative as a consequence of noise fluctuations, they were automatically set to zero at this stage of HS correction.
    \item \textit{spikes and drops identification}, which consisted in finding such numerical artifact and reclassifying them as missing data, in order to 
    avoid that such errors influenced data quality of the final dataset. A particular effort was devoted to identify and correct spiked higher than 
    $\SI{80}{\centi\meter}$ and snow depth drops of more than $\SI{50}{\centi\meter}$.
    \item \textit{gap filling}, which consisted in filling each gap which was located within numerical values both bigger than zero by means of a linear 
    interpolation of the endpoints. Clearly, this methodology does not allow for an accurate reconstruction of the snow depth on a given day within the 
    reading gap, but nevertheless makes it possible to distinguish whether the ground is bare or covered with snow. Requiring both endpoints bigger than 
    zero reduces the number of instances where the reconstruction is flawed, assigning numerical values to days when the presence of snow is uncertain. 
    \item \textit{first visual inspection}, in order to ensure that the gap filling was done correctly and to evaluate how to proceed with the organization 
    of the snow depth series. If the gap filling procedure resulted in faulty reconstructions, the previous step is done again changing the gap filling 
    parameters, in order to avoid erroneous outputs. 
    \item \textit{gap to zero}, which consisted in zeroing all the remaining missing data. This step, although it seems a bit rough, doesn't actually 
    introduce numerical artifacts, since series with gaps substantially affecting the start or the end of the snow season have already been discarded 
    at the first step. The worst-case scenario is that, at most, a couple of days per hydrological year are forced to zero due to the procedure just 
    described.
    \item \textit{zero under threshold}, which was done only in case the first visual inspection had highlighted the need to remove further (and not 
    overly significant) noise. In practice, all days whose HS falls below a certain threshold, typically a couple of centimeters, are set to zero.
    \item \textit{final visual inspection}, done in order to assess whether the outputs of the snow depth correction procedure are plausible. If some issues 
    are detected, they are handled singularly in order to ensure the successful outcome of the entire procedure.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Final dataset}

The two procedures developed to improve the dataset quality resulted in the removal of \num{220} stations, reducing the total number from 1052 to 832. 
Furthermore, the total number of hydrological years decreased from \num{27388} to \num{20248}, representing a \num{26}\% reduction. The spatial 
distribution of snow depth stations didn't change substantially, as they were moved at most by $\SI{1.5}{\kilo\meter}$. A sensible change can be instead 
detected on the number of station whose metadata are deemed as reliable by comparing them with the ALOS AW3D30 DEM, as shown in Figure 
\ref{fig:station_compatibility_before_after_QC}. In fact, on the total of 832 final stations, as many as 714 passed the consistency check, and are 
consequently colored green in panel (b) of the same figure. Regarding the elevation distribution, no major changes are detected. In fact, both original 
and corrected networks are more focused on the medium elevations, with the most represented elevation band being the one comprised between \num{1000} and 
\SI{2000}{\meter} a.s.l.

erroneous points. The outcome of the work is positive, in the sense that what can be observed in Figure \ref{fig:comparison_HS_MODIS_corrected} is much

\section{Calibration procedure}

The previously performed correction of the station metadata is particularly relevant at this stage, as accurate knowledge of the stations' geographical 
coordinates is essential to ensure proper calibration. From now on, only the 714 reliable stations of the 832 constituing the corrected dataset were 
taken into account. For each of their coordinates, a reconstruction of daily temperature and precipitation fields over the 1951--2023 period was attempted. 
This procedure was successful for only 632 station out of the 714, since in some cases there were too few data to obtain reliable outputs. Having this 
temperature and precipitation data made it possible to reconstruct SWE series for each of the remaining stations, which could then be compared with the 
corresponding snow depth series, since both quantify the amount of snow within the snowpack, but through different unit of measurements. However, the 
conversion from one to another is not straightforward, since it requires the knowledge of the vertical profile of snow density, which in the framework of 
this thesis remains unknown. This issue can be solved by means of a snow model such as $\Delta$Snow, which makes the conversion from snow depth to 
snow water equivalent under the assumption that every increase of more than a threshold $\tau$ in snow depth can be interpreted as a snowfall event. This 
multi layered simulation of the snowpack even predicts snow compaction by mean of a Newtonian approach. Within the framework of this thesis, however,
